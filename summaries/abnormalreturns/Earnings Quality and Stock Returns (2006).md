# Earnings Quality and Stock Returns
**Authors:** Louis K. C. Chan, Konan Chan, Narasimhan Jegadeesh, Josef Lakonishok
**Year:** 2006
**Journal/Venue:** Journal of Business

## Problem statement

The paper asks why accruals predict future stock returns. The standard explanation is managerial earnings manipulation, but that is not the only possibility. High accruals can also reflect temporarily deteriorating business conditions, or they can proxy for past growth that investors extrapolate too far.

The paper's objective is therefore not merely to re-document Sloan's anomaly. It is to discriminate among competing mechanisms by unpacking accruals into components, discretionary versus nondiscretionary pieces, and associated operating-performance patterns.

## Approach (short)

The authors define accruals as

$$
Accruals = DCA - DCL - DEP,
$$

where each term is scaled by average total assets. They then:

1. sort firms on accruals and study future returns;
2. interact accruals with earnings-surprise sorts;
3. decompose accruals into components such as receivables, inventory, and payables;
4. split each component into discretionary and nondiscretionary parts using a sales-based benchmark:

$$
E_t(I_{it})=\left[\frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=1}^5 \frac{I_{i,t-k}}{Sales_{i,t-k}}\right] Sales_{it},
$$

$$
NDI_{it}=E_t(I_{it})-I_{i,t-1},\qquad DI_{it}=I_{it}-E_t(I_{it}).
$$

The paper finds that the predictive power comes mainly from the discretionary component, especially inventory-related accruals, and that high-accrual firms later report increasingly negative special items.

## Approach (detailed)

### 1. Define accruals and the basic annual trading design

The sample is domestic common stocks on NYSE, AMEX, and Nasdaq excluding financial firms. Variables are measured at the end of April each year from 1971 to 1995, imposing a four-month reporting lag from fiscal year-end.

Accruals are defined as:

$$
Accruals = DCA - DCL - DEP,
$$

where:

- `DCA` = change in noncash current assets,
- `DCL` = change in current liabilities excluding short-term debt and taxes payable,
- `DEP` = depreciation and amortization.

Everything is scaled by average total assets.

At each annual formation date, firms are ranked on accruals and assigned to equally weighted decile portfolios. The paper then computes buy-and-hold raw returns and size/book-to-market-adjusted abnormal returns over each of the next three years.

### 2. Refine the standard earnings-surprise strategy using accruals

The first methodological point is that earnings surprises are more informative when conditioned on earnings quality. The paper independently sorts firms by:

- earnings change or earnings surprise,
- and accruals.

This produces a two-way classification. The design asks whether high earnings growth means the same thing when it is backed by cash flows versus when it is generated by accruals.

The answer is no. High earnings improvements accompanied by high accruals produce much weaker subsequent returns. The paper's practical message is that an earnings-surprise strategy should be filtered by accrual quality.

### 3. Track operating performance before and after high-accrual years

The paper then studies the time path of operating performance around the accrual event year. High-accrual firms display:

- strong past stock returns,
- strong past earnings growth,
- and rapid accrual buildup right before formation.

But after portfolio formation their operating momentum fades and future returns are poor. This time-series profile is consistent with the idea that the high-accrual year is a turning point rather than just a continuation of steady growth.

### 4. Examine individual accrual components

Accruals are broken into economically interpretable pieces, especially:

- `DAR`: change in accounts receivable,
- `DINV`: change in inventory,
- `DAP`: change in accounts payable.

Cross-sectional return regressions show that the components are not equally informative. Inventory changes have the strongest predictive power for future returns. This is important because it cuts against a catchall interpretation of accruals: some components carry much more information about future disappointment than others.

### 5. Separate discretionary from nondiscretionary accruals

The paper's central identification device is a parsimonious decomposition based on sales. For a generic working-capital item `I_{it}`, expected nondiscretionary level is:

$$
E_t(I_{it})=\left[\frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=1}^5 \frac{I_{i,t-k}}{Sales_{i,t-k}}\right] Sales_{it}.
$$

Then the nondiscretionary and discretionary changes are:

$$
NDI_{it}=E_t(I_{it})-I_{i,t-1},
$$

$$
DI_{it}=I_{it}-E_t(I_{it}).
$$

This is a simple but powerful design. If the extrapolation story were right, the **nondiscretionary**, sales-related component should predict future returns because it captures growth that investors overextrapolate. If the earnings-management story were right, the predictive content should sit mainly in the **discretionary** component.

The empirical result strongly favors the latter. The discretionary part, especially discretionary inventory buildup, drives the anomaly; the nondiscretionary part has little or no predictive power.

### 6. Measure the size of the discretionary-component effect directly

The extreme-decile spread is especially large for discretionary inventory:

- first-year abnormal return spread of about `9.1%` between low and high discretionary-inventory deciles.

This is one of the paper's strongest results because it isolates a very specific balance-sheet component and still gets a large return spread.

### 7. Track special items as footprints of prior overstatement

If high accruals partly reflect manipulation or delayed recognition of bad news, then future income-decreasing special items should become more negative after the high-accrual year. The paper therefore follows special items from three years before to three years after portfolio formation.

That test is informative because special items are one common channel through which previously hidden bad news is later recognized. The paper finds that the deterioration in special items is especially pronounced for the high-accrual group, which supports the earnings-management interpretation.

### 8. Build explicit return-prediction models from accrual components

The authors then estimate cross-sectional return regressions and use the coefficients as portfolio-sorting weights. Four models are considered:

1. all raw accrual components;
2. discretionary versions of those components;
3. a parsimonious model using inventory, receivables, and payables;
4. the discretionary versions of those three variables.

At each year-end, the paper takes rolling averages of prior regression coefficients, computes each stock's predicted return, sorts stocks into deciles, and holds them for the next year.

This is important methodologically: the paper shows that accruals are not just a stylized anomaly variable. One can explicitly build a better return predictor by weighting the informative components more heavily than the uninformative ones.

### 9. Check within-industry and international robustness

The paper also forms quartile sorts within industries and replicates the analysis in U.K. data. These tests matter because:

- accrual norms differ structurally across industries,
- and the anomaly could in principle be a U.S.-specific accounting artifact.

The return predictability survives both checks. Inventory-related accruals remain especially important.

### 10. What a reader should implement

A replication requires:

1. annual accrual computation with an April formation date;
2. decile sorts on total accruals;
3. a two-way sort on earnings change and accruals;
4. component-level regressions for receivables, inventory, and payables;
5. the sales-based discretionary/nondiscretionary decomposition above;
6. rolling-coefficient prediction models for future returns;
7. special-item tracking around the formation year.

The paper's main contribution is that it turns "accruals predict returns" into a structured methodology for identifying **which accruals**, and **which part of those accruals**, actually matter.

## Domain of applicability

- **Where it works well:** Equity universes with rich annual accounting data and consistent working-capital line items.
- **What is implementable:** Earnings-quality screens, discretionary-accrual signals, and component-level return predictors.
- **Main limitation:** Any discretionary/nondiscretionary split is model-dependent, so the decomposition is informative but not exact.
- **Why the paper matters:** It shows that the accrual anomaly is driven much more by specific, discretionary working-capital components than by a generic catchall accrual measure.
