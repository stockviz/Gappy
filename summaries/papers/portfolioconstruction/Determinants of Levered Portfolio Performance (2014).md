# 1. Metadata

- **Title:** Determinants of Levered Portfolio Performance
- **Author(s):** Robert M. Anderson, Stephen W. Bianchi, Lisa R. Goldberg
- **Year:** 2014
- **Journal/Venue:** Forthcoming in *Financial Analysts Journal* at the time of the manuscript

# 2. Problem statement

The paper asks how leverage affects realized portfolio performance over multiple periods. Specifically: **what determines the arithmetic and geometric return of a levered strategy built from a fully invested source portfolio, and what role is played by the covariance between leverage and the source portfolio’s excess return?**

# 3. Approach (short)

The paper derives exact arithmetic-return decompositions for levered strategies and then combines them with a geometric-return approximation to account for compounding. The novel term is a covariance correction between leverage and the source portfolio’s excess borrowing return. This term is especially important for dynamically levered, volatility-targeting strategies, because leverage moves inversely with estimated volatility and often covaries negatively with subsequent excess returns.

# 4. Approach (detailed)

1. **Single-period levered return**

   Let $r^S$ be the return on the fully invested source portfolio, $r^b$ the borrowing rate, and $\lambda$ the leverage ratio. The single-period return to the levered strategy is
   $$
   r^L=\lambda r^S-(\lambda-1)r^b.
   $$
   Excess return relative to the risk-free rate $r^f$ is
   $$
   r^L-r^f
   =
   \lambda(r^S-r^f)-(\lambda-1)(r^b-r^f).
   $$
   If $r^b>r^f$, Sharpe ratio declines with leverage even in a one-period setting.

2. **Arithmetic-return decomposition**

   Rewriting,
   $$
   r^L=r^S+(\lambda-1)(r^S-r^b).
   $$
   Taking expectations over time yields
   $$
   E[r^L]
   =
   E[r^S]
   +
   E[\lambda-1]\,E[r^S-r^b]
   +
   \operatorname{cov}(\lambda,r^S-r^b).
   $$
   This is the paper’s central formula. The first two terms are the **magnified source return**. The third is the newly emphasized covariance term.

3. **Adding trading costs**

   With turnover-induced trading costs,
   $$
   E[r^L]
   =
   E[r^S]
   +
   E[\lambda-1]E[r^S-r^b]
   +
   \operatorname{cov}(\lambda,r^S-r^b)
   -
   E[r_{TC}^S+r_{TC}^L].
   $$
   The paper separates trading cost due to turnover in the source portfolio from trading cost generated by leverage rebalancing itself.

4. **Why the covariance term appears**

   In a fixed-leverage strategy, $\lambda$ is constant, so
   $$
   \operatorname{cov}(\lambda,r^S-r^b)=0.
   $$
   In a dynamic-leverage strategy, especially volatility targeting, $\lambda_t$ varies over time. Since leverage is often increased after low-volatility periods and reduced after high-volatility periods, it can covary negatively with the source portfolio’s subsequent excess return.

   The paper’s two-period example makes this exact. When leverage rises before a bad return and falls before a good one, arithmetic mean return is reduced by the negative covariance term even if average leverage looks reasonable.

5. **Compounding and variance drag**

   Arithmetic expected return is not the full story. For geometric return the paper uses the approximation
   $$
   G[r]\approx (1+E[r])e^{-\operatorname{var}(r)/2}-1.
   $$
   Thus compounding imposes a variance drag, and leverage magnifies that drag because strategy variance rises with leverage. Dynamic leverage can therefore hurt performance through two distinct channels:

   - variance drag;
   - covariance drag.

6. **Fixed vs dynamic leverage**

   The decomposition clarifies an important distinction:

   - **fixed leverage:** no covariance drag, but still financing cost and variance drag;
   - **dynamic leverage / volatility targeting:** all of the above, plus covariance drag.

   This is why a volatility-targeted strategy can underperform a fixed-leverage strategy even if both target similar long-run volatility.

7. **Empirical implication**

   In the historical examples studied by the paper, the covariance term is negative over long horizons for several volatility-targeting strategies, substantially lowering realized return and Sharpe ratio. The point is not that the covariance must always be negative, but that it is economically large enough that ignoring it is a material error.

8. **Proof logic**

   The paper’s formulas are exact algebra, not asymptotics. Starting from
   $$
   r^L=r^S+(\lambda-1)(r^S-r^b),
   $$
   use the identity
   $$
   E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]+\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)
   $$
   with $X=\lambda-1$, $Y=r^S-r^b$. The geometric-return approximation is the standard lognormal-style approximation based on the effect of volatility on compound growth.

**Additional mathematical details**

The paper’s exact arithmetic decomposition is
$$
r_t^L
=
r_t^S+(\lambda_t-1)(r_t^S-r_t^b),
$$
so averaging over time gives
$$
E[r^L]
=
E[r^S]
+E[\lambda-1]\,E[r^S-r^b]
+\operatorname{cov}(\lambda,r^S-r^b).
$$
The covariance term is therefore not a higher-order approximation; it is an exact component of average arithmetic performance whenever leverage varies through time.

The geometric-return analysis is the approximate part. Using the usual log-return expansion,
$$
g \approx E[r]-\frac12 \operatorname{Var}(r),
$$
the paper shows that dynamic leverage can lower compound growth through two distinct channels: variance drag and covariance drag. Fixed leverage eliminates the second term mechanically because $\lambda_t$ is constant; volatility targeting does not.

# 5. Domain of applicability

- The framework applies to **levered strategies built from a fully invested source portfolio**.
- It is especially relevant for **volatility-targeting** rules because those generate time-varying leverage.
- The arithmetic decomposition is exact; the geometric-return formula is an approximation.
- The paper does not prove that covariance drag is always negative. That sign is empirical and strategy dependent.
- Its strongest contribution is analytical: any serious evaluation of levered portfolio performance must account for leverage-return covariance, not just average leverage.
