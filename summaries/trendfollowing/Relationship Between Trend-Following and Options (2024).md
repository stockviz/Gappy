# Relationship Between Trend-Following and Options
**Authors:** Vineer Bhansali, Jeremie Holdom
**Year:** 2024
**Journal/Venue:** LongTail Alpha research note

## Problem statement

Trend-following and option-based hedging are both used as crisis diversifiers, but they appear to work through different instruments. Trend-following trades linear futures and swaps and earns from realized moves. Options embed nonlinearity contractually and react immediately to implied-volatility changes. The note asks: **how deep is the analogy between the two, and can one use that analogy to design better hybrid risk-mitigation portfolios?** In particular, the authors care about reversal risk in trend following and the cost inefficiency of static option hedges.

## Approach (short)

The note interprets trend-following as a delta-replication program for option payoff ranges. A straddle defines a target convex payoff; a trend rule attempts to replicate that payoff by dynamically changing directional exposure. The replication earns the desired payoff range minus quadratic-variation costs, while an outright option buyer pays time decay instead. Dynamic or path-dependent strikes, such as lookback or rolling straddles, correspond to moving trend filters such as moving averages. Signal horizon maps naturally into option tenor: short-term trend resembles high-gamma short-dated options, while long-term trend resembles low-gamma long-dated options. The note then uses this mapping to derive practical design rules for monetization, confirmation thresholds, stop losses, and option overlays for reversal control.

## Approach (detailed)

### 1. Conceptual equivalence: both strategies increase exposure into moves

The authors start from the simplest common feature. Trend followers:

- increase long exposure in rising markets,
- increase short exposure in falling markets.

Option deltas behave the same way:

- a call's delta rises as spot rises,
- a put's absolute delta rises as spot falls.

So both strategies are pro-cyclical in exposure. The difference is where the convexity comes from:

- for options, convexity is in the contract;
- for trend following, convexity is in the trading rule.

This reframes trend following as a dynamic replication problem rather than a purely statistical forecasting rule.

### 2. Straddle payoff range as the target object

Let
$$
\phi_t(S_t,T) = C_t(S_t,T) + P_t(S_t,T)
$$
be the price of an at-the-money straddle. Define the payoff range
$$
R(S_T,T) = \phi_0(S_T,T) - \phi_0(S_0,T).
$$

This is not the outright straddle P&L after theta decay. It is the *range* the option's marked value would attain if one shocked spot to $S_T$ while holding maturity fixed. That is the right object to compare with a trading rule because it isolates the convex spot dependence from pure time decay.

A trend program is then interpreted as trying to replicate this range dynamically.

### 3. Delta replication and quadratic-variation costs

If the replicating strategy holds
$$
\Delta_t = \frac{\partial \phi_0(S_t,T)}{\partial S_t},
$$
its cumulative replication P&L is approximately
$$
PNL^R_t
=
\sum_{s=1}^t \Delta_{s-1}(S_s-S_{s-1})
\approx
R(S_t,T) - \frac12\sum_{s=1}^t \Gamma_{s-1}(S_s-S_{s-1})^2.
$$

So the replication achieves the desired convex payoff *minus* gamma-slippage costs. This is the key mechanical analogy:

- options: pay theta up front, avoid dynamic slippage;
- trend following: avoid explicit theta, but pay realized whipsaw and transaction costs generated by quadratic variation.

The note interprets this as the natural mapping from option Greeks to trend mechanics:

- **delta** -> directional exposure rule,
- **gamma** -> speed with which the rule changes exposure,
- **theta** -> the unavoidable cost of maintaining convexity, which appears in trend following as whipsaw and turnover rather than option decay,
- **vega** -> immediate sensitivity to implied-volatility repricing, which explicit options have and pure trend following largely does not.

### 4. Replicating seasoned straddles versus fixed-range payoffs

If instead one updates the replication delta using the *current* option maturity,
$$
\Delta_t = \partial \phi_t(S_t,T)/\partial S_t,
$$
the resulting strategy tracks the actual straddle P&L minus theta and gamma costs. The note's point is that different trend rules correspond to different replication conventions:

- fixed-strike constant-maturity replication,
- calendar-restruck replication,
- dynamically re-centered replication.

These are design choices, not just implementation details.

### 5. Why lookback straddles match moving trend filters

Fung and Hsieh's lookback-straddle idea is restated in more operational terms. A lookback straddle profits from the range
$$
S_{\max} - S_{\min}
$$
over the horizon. Goldman et al.'s replication result says one can construct it by holding two straddles and re-striking them whenever the market makes a new high or low.

That is very close to what a trend filter does:

- it continually updates the effective threshold around which exposure flips,
- it embeds path dependence because the signal depends on the route taken by prices, not just the endpoint.

Thus a moving average acts like a dynamically updated strike. This is the note's main theoretical bridge between option path dependence and practical trend rules.

### 6. Signal horizon maps into option tenor

The authors then compare trend signals of different lengths with straddles of different maturities.

Their simulation message is simple:

- short-term trend signals profit most from autocorrelation and behave like short-dated, high-gamma options;
- long-term trend signals profit most from drift and behave like longer-dated, lower-gamma options;
- intermediate cases sit between the two.

This mapping is useful because it tells you what kind of market behavior a given trend rule is implicitly designed to monetize. A 10-day signal is not just a "faster trend". It is economically closer to holding a short-dated option-replication scheme with high gamma and high whipsaw risk. A 200-day signal is closer to holding a longer-dated option exposure with lower gamma and greater tolerance for local noise.

### 7. Practical use cases derived from the mapping

The note's most original part is not the basic analogy but the list of portfolio-construction consequences.

**1. Periodic monetization.**  
If one re-strikes or seasons the synthetic option rather than holding a fixed constant-maturity target, the analogue in trend following is profit taking. The claim is that some monetization is generally superior to none because otherwise strong gains are surrendered during reversals.

**2. Signal confirmation.**  
Moving from straddles to strangles corresponds to entry thresholds. You do not take the trade until the move is large enough to justify it. That lowers false positives but gives up early profits.

**3. Stop losses.**  
A stop-loss rule is compared to a knock-out option. The point is not cosmetic. A stop loss hides an embedded short jump or short tail position at the barrier. That means stops are not a costless risk-control device; they exchange one form of risk for another.

**4. Reversal-risk hedging.**  
This is the main application the authors care about. A long-horizon trend program can be kept intact while shorter-dated options are used to insure against sharp countertrend reversals. The longer-dated synthetic exposure keeps harvesting the slow variance expansion; the short-dated explicit options protect against the short-horizon gamma pain.

## Domain of applicability

- **Where it works well:** The note is strongest as a design framework. It gives a common language for discussing trend rules and option overlays in the same payoff geometry.
- **What is implementable:** The implementation ideas are concrete: choose trend horizon as if choosing option tenor; use explicit profit-taking rules as a re-striking schedule; treat confirmation bands as strangles; view stop losses as barrier options with hidden tail costs; consider short-dated options to hedge reversal risk in long-horizon trend programs.
- **Main limitations:** This is a practitioner note, not a full structural model. The replication formulas are approximate, the market-dynamics exercises are simulated, and the empirical evidence is illustrative rather than exhaustive.
- **What it does not prove:** It does not prove that one specific hybrid trend-plus-options architecture dominates. It shows which trade-offs exist and how to reason about them in Greek language.
- **What is genuinely novel:** The novel contribution is the systematic mapping from option design choices to trend-following design choices. Earlier work mostly stopped at "trend following is like a straddle." This note goes further and asks what option tenor, moneyness, theta, gamma, monetization, and barriers mean for actual trend-system construction.
