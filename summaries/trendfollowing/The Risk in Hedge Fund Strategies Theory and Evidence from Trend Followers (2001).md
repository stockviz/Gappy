# The Risk in Hedge Fund Strategies: Theory and Evidence from Trend Followers
**Authors:** William Fung, David A. Hsieh
**Year:** 2001
**Journal/Venue:** *Review of Financial Studies*, Vol. 14, No. 2

## Problem statement

Linear factor models built on standard stock, bond, currency, and commodity indices explain almost none of the variation in commodity trading advisor (CTA) returns. This has often been interpreted as evidence that trend-following funds have little systematic risk and generate "alpha". Fung and Hsieh argue that this is the wrong benchmark. The right question is not whether trend followers load linearly on passive asset indices, but whether their nonlinear trading rules are better represented as contingent claims on those same markets. The paper therefore asks: **can trend-following fund returns be understood as the returns of an option-like trading strategy, and if so which option structure best captures the economics of the strategy?**

## Approach (short)

The paper defines a primitive market-timing strategy (PMTS) and a primitive trend-following strategy (PTFS). PMTS tries to capture the net move between the initial and terminal price over a horizon, so its perfect-foresight payoff is $|S' - S|$ and is naturally associated with a standard straddle. PTFS instead tries to capture the largest directional move that occurs *within* the horizon, so its perfect-foresight payoff is $S_{\max} - S_{\min}$ and is naturally associated with a lookback straddle. The authors then build historical PTFS return series by replicating three-month lookback straddles across 26 global futures markets and show that these option-based benchmarks explain CTA returns far better than standard asset indices.

## Approach (detailed)

### 1. Primitive market timing versus primitive trend following

The paper starts from the Merton market-timing setup. Over a fixed interval, a perfect market timer who can go long or short captures the absolute terminal move:
$$
|S' - S|.
$$
This is the payoff of a standard straddle, ignoring the financing leg.

Trend following is different. A trend follower is not just trying to predict the sign of the *net* change between start and finish. It tries to harvest a sustained move that may start, reverse, and restart inside the interval. Fung and Hsieh therefore define the perfect-foresight primitive trend-following payoff as
$$
S_{\max} - S_{\min},
$$
the full range traversed by the asset during the window.

This distinction is the conceptual core of the paper. If the high and low occur at the ends of the interval, the PMTS and PTFS coincide. If the high and low occur inside the interval, the lookback-style PTFS dominates. That is why a lookback straddle is theoretically closer to trend following than a standard straddle.

### 2. Why the lookback straddle matches the economics of trend following

The authors characterize the PMTS as a long standard straddle and the PTFS as a long lookback straddle. The key argument is path dependence.

- A standard straddle depends only on the terminal price relative to the initial strike.
- A lookback straddle depends on the running maximum and minimum over the life of the trade.

That second object is much closer to the "buy breakouts, sell breakdowns" behavior of real CTAs. As prices make new highs, the strategy effectively ratchets exposure upward; as they make new lows, it ratchets exposure downward. Goldman, Sosin, and Gatto's replication argument is therefore central: a lookback straddle can be replicated by holding two standard straddles and rolling the strike upward when price makes a new high and downward when it makes a new low. This rollover rule is the options-market analogue of trend-followers updating positions as trends extend.

The paper also writes the deltas of the standard straddle and lookback straddle explicitly. The exact formulas matter less than the qualitative implication: the lookback straddle's delta flips more aggressively after new extrema because the contract is path-dependent. That is precisely the extra responsiveness one wants from a trend benchmark.

### 3. Empirical construction of the PTFS database

The empirical implementation uses 26 futures markets:

- stock indices,
- government bonds,
- three-month interest rates,
- currencies,
- commodities.

Because exchange-traded lookback options do not exist, the authors replicate them by rolling ordinary at-the-money straddles. They use:

- three-month maturity options, largely because quarterly expiries are the deepest and most consistently available,
- daily monitoring and rolling,
- monthly aggregation of returns to line up with the hedge fund reporting frequency.

Several implementation details matter:

1. The replication includes cash flows generated by strike rolls.
2. The study uses nearest-to-the-money listed options when exact ATM strikes are unavailable.
3. American-style options are treated as if they were European for practical purposes, which biases PTFS returns somewhat downward.
4. Transaction costs from the option replication are not modeled in full detail.

The point of the construction is not that actual CTAs literally buy lookback straddles. It is that one can price a *benchmark* for the opportunity set of perfect trend following using observable option markets.

### 4. Distributional facts: positive skewness is the first signature

The first empirical result is distributional rather than regression-based. Trend-following fund returns are strongly positively skewed. PTFS returns are also strongly positively skewed, across individual markets and across the five broad PTFS portfolios.

This matters because a linear factor model can easily miss the risk of a strategy whose payoff is nonlinear and state-dependent. Positive skewness is already a strong hint that the economically relevant risk factor is option-like rather than beta-like.

### 5. Standard benchmarks fail; PTFS benchmarks work

The paper then runs the benchmarking horse race.

Regressing representative trend-following fund returns on the standard eight major asset classes from Fung and Hsieh (1997a) produces essentially no explanatory power. Similar failure occurs if one uses stock indices only, bond indices only, currency markets only, commodity indices, or industry benchmarks such as the GSCI, CRB, or Mount Lucas/BARRA trend index.

By contrast, regressing trend-following fund returns on the five PTFS portfolios yields an adjusted $R^2$ of about 48%. The currency and commodity PTFS portfolios contribute the most explanatory power, with bonds and rates also important. This is the main empirical claim of the paper: **trend followers do have systematic risk, but it is nonlinear systematic risk that only becomes visible when the benchmarks themselves have option-like payoffs.**

### 6. Extreme-equity states and the "long event risk" interpretation

The authors then sort months by world equity performance into five states. Trend-following funds perform best in the extreme states, especially when world equities are either very bad or very good. PTFS portfolios on stocks, bonds, currencies, and short rates show the same basic behavior.

This is the operational meaning of being "long volatility" or "long market event risk" in the paper:

- trend-followers are not strongly exposed to the *average* monthly move in equity markets,
- but they tend to profit when markets experience large dislocations.

The important nuance is that this does not primarily come from direct exposure to stock-index straddles. The preferred-habitat regressions suggest that, during extreme equity episodes, the returns of trend-followers are more closely connected to PTFS exposures in:

- U.S. bonds,
- Eurodollar and Short Sterling,
- deutschemark and Japanese yen,
- wheat and silver.

So the convex payoff in crises is multi-asset, not simply an equity put.

### 7. Why standard and lookback straddles are hard to distinguish empirically

One subtle result is that, in the monthly data and with quarterly option expiries, standard straddles and lookback straddles are empirically quite correlated. That means the paper's empirical results do not prove that actual CTAs are *uniquely* lookback-like. Instead, the authors' claim is narrower and stronger: among simple option-based benchmarks, the lookback straddle is the correct theoretical primitive for trend following, and option-like benchmarks dominate linear benchmarks empirically.

## Domain of applicability

- **Where it works well:** The paper is strongest as a benchmark-design argument. If the object is to measure what kind of nonlinear market exposure a trend follower has, the PTFS is far more informative than a regression on passive asset indices.
- **What it actually proves:** It proves that option-like benchmarks explain CTA returns much better than standard asset returns. It does not prove that any real manager literally trades the PTFS or that the PTFS is the unique correct benchmark for every CTA.
- **Main limitations:** The PTFS is a perfect-foresight primitive, not a realistic alpha model. The empirical construction relies on three-month option data, monthly fund returns, approximate ATM strikes, and an option-rolling implementation that omits some frictions. The paper also abstracts from manager heterogeneity; its own conclusion is that one universal benchmark for all trend followers is unlikely.
- **Practical implementation takeaway:** If you want to benchmark or risk-manage a trend-following fund, you should build benchmarks out of path-dependent option-like factors on the markets the manager trades, not from linear exposures to buy-and-hold indices.
- **What is genuinely novel:** The paper's real contribution is the PTFS concept. It takes the vague statement that "trend followers are long volatility" and turns it into a concrete, tradable benchmark based on lookback straddles, then shows empirically that this benchmark recovers systematic structure that linear models miss.
