# Dynamic Volume-Return Relation of Individual Stocks
**Authors:** Guillermo Llorente, Roni Michaely, Gideon Saar, Jiang Wang
**Year:** 2002
**Journal/Venue:** Review of Financial Studies

## Problem statement

This paper is not a standard winner-minus-loser study. Its question is more primitive and more useful for momentum theory: **when does a return shock reverse, and when does it continue, once we condition on trading volume and on the motive for trade?**

The paper's claim is that the sign of short-horizon continuation depends on the mix of:

- hedging or risk-sharing trades, which push prices away from fundamentals and then reverse;
- speculative trades based on private information, which move prices toward information that is only partially impounded and therefore continue.

That makes the paper a mechanism paper for momentum rather than a portfolio-sorting paper.

## Approach (short)

The authors build a parsimonious equilibrium model with two investor classes:

- one class that trades for hedging reasons because of nontraded risk;
- one class that also receives private information about future payoffs.

From that model they derive a conditional return equation in which future return depends on the interaction of current return and current volume. The key comparative-static result is that the coefficient on the volume-return interaction becomes more positive as information asymmetry rises.

They then estimate, stock by stock,

$$
R_{i,t+1}=C_{0,i}+C_{1,i}R_{i,t}+C_{2,i}V_{i,t}R_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t+1},
$$

using daily data for NYSE and AMEX stocks from 1993 to 1998, and test whether `C2` is more positive for stocks with greater information asymmetry, proxied by bid-ask spreads, market capitalization, and analyst following.

## Approach (detailed)

### 1. Build a model in which the same price move can mean either reversal or continuation

The model is designed to separate two trading motives:

- **hedging trade** generated by changing endowment risk;
- **speculative trade** generated by private information about future dividends.

The same observed negative return can therefore mean very different things:

- if it came from hedging sales, price moved away from unchanged fundamentals and should partially reverse;
- if it came from informed sales, price only partially incorporated bad news and should continue downward when that news is revealed more fully.

That is the paper's central identification idea.

### 2. Use volume to identify trade-generated returns

Public news can move price without necessarily generating trade. By contrast, both hedging trade and speculative trade produce volume. The paper therefore uses volume as an imperfect but useful signal that the return was generated by trading rather than by pure public-news repricing.

The model implies that conditioning on current return alone is too coarse. Conditioning on the **return-volume pair** is what separates likely reversal episodes from likely continuation episodes.

### 3. Derive the conditional return equation

After solving the equilibrium and normalizing volume by its unconditional mean, the paper obtains a nonlinear conditional-expectation equation of the form

$$
E[R_{t+1}\mid \widetilde V_t,R_t]
=
\text{function of } R_t \text{ and } \widetilde V_t R_t.
$$

For empirical implementation, the paper linearizes the relation around small daily returns and volumes and motivates the regression

$$
R_{i,t+1}=C_{0,i}+C_{1,i}R_{i,t}+C_{2,i}V_{i,t}R_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
$$

The interpretation is:

- `C1` captures ordinary first-order autocorrelation when volume is negligible;
- `C2` captures how volume changes that autocorrelation.

The theoretical comparative static is the important piece: as information asymmetry rises, `C2` should become more positive. Intuitively, high volume is then more likely to reflect informed speculative trade rather than pure hedging trade.

### 4. State the cross-sectional prediction in terms of information asymmetry

The model uses the variance of private information as the latent information-asymmetry parameter. That object is not observable, so the empirical design replaces it with stock-level proxies.

The paper's testable implication is monotone:

- higher information asymmetry `=>` more speculative trading in high-volume periods;
- more speculative trading `=>` weaker reversal or stronger continuation after high-volume returns;
- therefore `C2` should be increasing in information asymmetry.

This is the prediction the empirical section takes to the data.

### 5. Construct daily return and volume measures carefully

The sample contains NYSE and AMEX common stocks from January 1993 to December 1998. To stabilize stock-level estimates, the authors keep firms that trade on at least 1000 of the 1516 trading days, leaving 2226 stocks.

The variables are built as follows:

- `R_{i,t}`: daily return from CRSP;
- turnover: shares traded divided by shares outstanding;
- volume regressor `V_{i,t}`: detrended log turnover, where the log turnover series is demeaned with a 200-trading-day moving average.

This matters because the paper wants a stationary stock-specific volume series rather than raw level turnover, which trends over time and differs sharply across firms.

### 6. Estimate the key time-series regression stock by stock

For each stock `i`, the paper runs:

$$
R_{i,t+1}=C_{0,i}+C_{1,i}R_{i,t}+C_{2,i}V_{i,t}R_{i,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
$$

This is a first-stage estimation. The object of interest is not the average `C2` alone, but the **cross-sectional dispersion in `C2`** across firms.

Interpretation:

- negative `C2`: high-volume returns are followed by stronger reversal;
- positive `C2`: high-volume returns are followed by weaker reversal or outright continuation.

That is the exact operational version of the model.

### 7. Relate `C2` to information-asymmetry proxies

The paper uses two main proxies in the baseline tests:

- average opening percentage bid-ask spread from TAQ;
- average market capitalization.

It ordinal-transforms the proxies to avoid imposing a particular functional form on the relation between the proxy and latent information asymmetry. Then it runs both:

- grouped comparisons across low, medium, and high asymmetry buckets;
- cross-sectional regressions of `C2_i` on the proxy rank.

The cross-sectional regression is

$$
C_{2,i}=a+bA_i+u_i,
$$

where `A_i` is the information-asymmetry proxy.

Predicted signs:

- `b > 0` when `A_i` is bid-ask spread;
- `b < 0` when `A_i` is size.

### 8. Interpret the estimates economically

The grouped and cross-sectional results line up with the model:

- small-cap or high-spread stocks have more positive `C2`;
- large-cap or low-spread stocks have more negative `C2`.

So the market does not display one universal volume-return pattern. It displays a pattern that depends on how likely high volume is to be informed.

### 9. Show that the effect is firm-specific, not just a marketwide artifact

The paper does not stop at raw returns and volumes. It decomposes both return and volume into systematic and unsystematic components using market-model style residualization and repeats the analysis.

This is a crucial robustness check. If the relation between `C2` and information asymmetry vanished after stripping out marketwide variation, the interpretation would weaken. Instead, the relation survives, which supports the claim that the mechanism is tied to firm-specific private information.

### 10. Address microstructure and measurement objections directly

The paper also checks that the result is not driven by:

- bid-ask bounce;
- the exact timing of the last daily trade;
- nonsynchronous trading;
- alternative volume definitions;
- alternative econometric estimators.

It additionally uses analyst following as a third information-asymmetry proxy. Stocks followed by more analysts exhibit less continuation after high-volume returns, again consistent with lower private-information intensity.

### 11. What a reader should implement

A faithful implementation is:

1. build daily stock returns and detrended log-turnover series;
2. estimate the stock-level interaction regression `R_{i,t+1}` on `R_{i,t}` and `V_{i,t}R_{i,t}`;
3. treat `C2_i` as the stock's continuation-versus-reversal loading on high-volume days;
4. explain `C2_i` in the cross section using asymmetry proxies such as spreads, size, or analyst following;
5. rerun using residualized returns and residualized volume to isolate firm-specific effects.

That is the methodological core of the paper.

## Domain of applicability

- **Where it works well:** Short-horizon studies of when price moves reverse or continue, especially when one wants a mechanism for momentum rooted in trading motives.
- **What is implementable:** A two-step design that first estimates stock-specific volume-return interaction coefficients and then explains them with information-asymmetry proxies.
- **Main limitation:** The paper is about daily continuation and reversal, not the classic six- to 12-month momentum sort directly.
- **Why the paper matters:** It gives a concrete microfoundation for why some high-volume return shocks reverse and others continue, which is exactly the kind of mechanism a serious momentum model needs.
