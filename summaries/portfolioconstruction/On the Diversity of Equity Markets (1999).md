# 1. Metadata

- **Title:** On the Diversity of Equity Markets
- **Author(s):** Robert Fernholz
- **Year:** 1999
- **Journal/Venue:** *Journal of Mathematical Economics*

# 2. Problem statement

The paper asks whether diversity of market capitalization is a natural property of an equity market modeled by continuous semimartingales, and what mechanisms are needed to maintain it. Formally, if market weights are
$$
\mu_i(t)=\frac{X_i(t)}{\sum_{j=1}^n X_j(t)},
$$
under what conditions can one prevent concentration $\mu_{(1)}(t)\to 1$, and how is diversity related to portfolio growth and equilibrium?

# 3. Approach (short)

The method is stochastic portfolio theory. Fernholz defines portfolios, excess growth rate, market diversity, and market entropy directly from the semimartingale structure of stock capitalizations. He then constructs the entropy-weighted portfolio and derives an exact pathwise decomposition of its relative log-return to the market. This links diversity to relative arbitrage-like outperformance and shows that dividend recycling is needed to sustain diversity under equilibrium.

# 4. Approach (detailed)

1. **Market model**

   Stock capitalizations $X_i$ are positive continuous semimartingales. A portfolio $p$ is a predictable weight process with $\sum_i p_i=1$. Its log-growth satisfies
   $$
   d\log Z_p = \sum_i p_i\,d\log X_i + g_p^*\,dt,
   $$
   where the excess growth rate $g_p^*$ is generated by diversification.

2. **Excess growth rate**

   A key deterministic-algebraic result is that for long-only portfolios $g_p^*\ge 0$. This is the mathematical statement that diversification reduces portfolio volatility relative to the weighted average volatility of constituents, and the saved variance reappears as growth.

3. **Market diversity**

   The market portfolio is $p=\mu$, the capitalization weights. Diversity means the largest weight stays uniformly away from one. Under the source’s uniform lower and upper covariance bounds, Fernholz shows this is equivalent to a strictly positive lower bound on market excess growth:
   $$
   \text{market diverse} \iff \exists d>0 \text{ such that } g_\mu^*(t)\ge d \ \forall t.
   $$
   Intuitively, diversity is exactly what allows the market to retain diversification-generated growth.

4. **Why diversity is not automatic**

   The paper proves that under equal total-growth assumptions, diversity is not preserved automatically. In a frictionless capital market, capital tends to concentrate unless some recycling mechanism pushes value back toward smaller firms.

5. **Dividends as recycling mechanism**

   Let $d_i$ denote stock dividend rates. If all stocks have the same total growth rate, then maintaining diversity requires dividends to be distributed in a way that effectively transfers capital from large firms to smaller ones. This is the paper’s core economic conclusion: dividend payouts can stabilize diversity.

6. **Entropy as a diversity measure**

   Market entropy is defined as
   $$
   S(t)=-\sum_{i=1}^n \mu_i(t)\log \mu_i(t).
   $$
   Proposition 4.1 states:
   $$
   \text{market diverse} \iff \exists \varepsilon>0 \text{ such that } S(t)\ge \varepsilon \ \forall t.
   $$
   So entropy is not just a descriptive statistic; it is equivalent to diversity in the model.

7. **Entropy-weighted portfolio**

   Define the entropy-weighted portfolio $h$ by
   $$
   h_i(t)= -\frac{\mu_i(t)\log \mu_i(t)}{S(t)}.
   $$
   Smaller firms receive relatively larger weights because the ratio $h_i/\mu_i$ falls with $\mu_i$.

8. **Master decomposition**

   Theorem 4.1 gives the exact pathwise identity
   $$
   d\log S(t)=d\log\!\left(\frac{Z_h(t)}{Z_\mu(t)}\right)-\frac{g_\mu^*(t)}{S(t)}\,dt.
   $$
   Rearranging,
   $$
   d\log\!\left(\frac{Z_h(t)}{Z_\mu(t)}\right)
   = d\log S(t) + \frac{g_\mu^*(t)}{S(t)}\,dt.
   $$
   This is the paper’s central exact formula. The relative performance of the entropy-weighted portfolio is driven by two terms:
   - change in diversity $d\log S(t)$,
   - accumulated market excess growth scaled by entropy.

9. **Implication for long-run relative performance**

   If the market remains diverse so that $S(t)$ stays bounded away from zero and $g_\mu^*(t)$ stays positive, then the drift term on the right-hand side is positive enough to imply
   $$
   \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_\mu(t)}{Z_h(t)}=0
   \quad\text{a.s.}
   $$
   Thus the entropy-weighted portfolio eventually dominates the market in relative wealth, pathwise.

10. **Equilibrium implication**

    In weak equilibrium, no portfolio should dominate the market portfolio. Combining this with the previous decomposition implies that if the market is diverse and in weak equilibrium, dividend rates of large stocks must exceed those of the entropy-weighted benchmark in the right way. This is how the paper connects entropy, dividends, and equilibrium.

# 5. Domain of applicability

- The diversity theorems apply to the source’s bounded-volatility, uniformly nondegenerate diffusion market and its long-only market/entropy portfolios.
- The pathwise decomposition is exact in that setting and does not require expected-return estimation.
- The equilibrium interpretation is weaker than modern no-arbitrage asset pricing; it is closer to “no dominating portfolio” than to equivalent martingale measure theory.
- The paper does not model entry, exit, mergers, or discrete corporate events, so its exact results are stylized relative to actual equity indices.
- The novel contribution is the entropy/diversity decomposition linking market structure to relative portfolio growth.


## 6. The assumptions behind the diversity equivalences

The source is *Journal of Mathematical Economics* 31 (1999), pp. 393–417. Its market class is narrower than an arbitrary collection of positive continuous semimartingales. Stock capitalizations satisfy

$$
d\log X_i(t)=g_i(t)\,dt+\sum_{\nu=1}^{n}\xi_{i\nu}(t)\,dW_\nu(t),
$$

with locally integrable growth processes and bounded volatility coefficients. The covariance matrix $a(t)=\xi(t)\xi(t)^\top$ is **uniformly nondegenerate**: for deterministic positive constants $\varepsilon,M$,

$$
\varepsilon\|z\|^2\le z^\top a(t)z\le M\|z\|^2
$$

for every vector $z$, uniformly in time almost surely. These lower and upper bounds do essential work. A market of perfectly correlated stocks could have stable, diverse weights but zero diversification excess growth; without nondegeneracy the stated equivalence would fail.

The list of firms and their share counts are fixed. Companies do not enter, exit, merge, or split; shares are divisible; trading is continuous and frictionless; dividends are represented by continuous rates. The paper acknowledges that more general models could be developed, but those extensions are not proved merely by stating the current results.

Portfolio weights are adapted, bounded processes summing to one. The general definition allows shorts, while nonnegativity of excess growth and the diversity arguments rely on long-only portfolios such as the market and entropy-weighted portfolio. The stochastic integrability conditions and boundedness also support the long-run martingale law used in the proofs.

## 7. Growth, arithmetic return, and diversification

The stock's arithmetic drift differs from its log-growth rate:

$$
\frac{dX_i}{X_i}=\left(g_i+\frac12a_{ii}\right)dt+
\sum_\nu\xi_{i\nu}\,dW_\nu.
$$

For a self-financing portfolio $\pi$,

$$
g_\pi=\sum_i\pi_i g_i+\gamma_\pi^*,\qquad
\gamma_\pi^*=\frac12\left(\sum_i\pi_i a_{ii}-\pi^\top a\pi\right).
$$

The excess-growth term compares portfolio variance with a weighted average of constituent variances. An alternative exact identity is

$$
\gamma_\pi^*=\frac14\sum_{i,j}\pi_i\pi_j
(a_{ii}+a_{jj}-2a_{ij}).
$$

For nonnegative weights, each pairwise relative-variance term contributes nonnegatively. This is an algebraic property of diversification within the specified diffusion model, not a free-standing excess return over cash or a claim that every diversified portfolio outperforms every constituent.

Relative covariance provides another useful representation. With market weights $\mu$, define

$$
\tau_{ij}^{\mu}=a_{ij}-a_{i\mu}-a_{j\mu}+a_{\mu\mu},
$$

where $a_{i\mu}=\sum_k\mu_k a_{ik}$. This is the instantaneous covariance of relative log capitalizations $\log(X_i/Z_\mu)$ and $\log(X_j/Z_\mu)$. Then $\gamma_\mu^*=\frac12\sum_i\mu_i\tau_{ii}^{\mu}$. The relevant quantity is cross-sectional relative variation, not simply total market volatility.

The paper proves that the martingale part of log portfolio wealth divided by time tends to zero almost surely. Thus long-run log wealth tracks the time integral of its growth-rate process, even when that process is time varying and no single limiting average is assumed to exist. This distinction avoids imposing an unnecessary IID return model.

## 8. Why common growth does not preserve diversity

Diversity means a deterministic $\delta>0$ exists such that $\max_i\mu_i(t)\le1-\delta$ for all time almost surely. With fixed finite $n$, this only prevents a single firm from approaching complete dominance. It does not require weights to be close to equal or guarantee that every firm retains a positive minimum share.

Under the bounded, nondegenerate covariance conditions, the paper proves that this condition is equivalent to a uniform positive lower bound on $\gamma_\mu^*$. The proof relates the excess-growth rate to $\mu_i(1-\mu_i)$ and uses that at least one weight is at least $1/n$. The upper covariance bound supplies the converse: as concentration approaches a vertex, excess growth must vanish.

Now suppose all firms share the same log-growth process $g(t)$ and pay no dividends. Each firm's log capitalization differs from $\int_0^t g(s)ds$ by a sublinear term. The total capitalization lies between $n\min_iX_i$ and $n\max_iX_i$, so it has the same asymptotic average growth. Yet its portfolio growth formula is $g+\gamma_\mu^*$. These facts force

$$
\frac1t\int_0^t\gamma_\mu^*(s)ds\longrightarrow0
\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$

A uniformly diverse market would require this average to remain bounded below, a contradiction. The same conclusion holds when individual growth rates are constant but differ: firms below the largest growth rate become asymptotically negligible, and the tied highest-growth submarket inherits the difficulty.

“Not diverse” must not be strengthened to “one named firm converges permanently to 100% market share.” The failure of a uniform concentration bound allows leadership to change and concentration to recur at different times. The theorem supplies the vanishing average excess-growth conclusion and the failure of the specified diversity property, not a unique eventual winning company in every model.

## 9. Entropy and the integrated master equation

Entropy $S(\mu)=-\sum_i\mu_i\log\mu_i$ lies in $(0,\log n]$ for strictly positive weights. On the closed simplex it vanishes only at the vertices. Continuity and compactness therefore identify a positive lower entropy bound with avoidance of those vertices. This part of the equivalence is geometric; the relationship with excess growth additionally uses the covariance conditions.

The entropy portfolio has weights $h_i=-\mu_i\log\mu_i/S$. Its relative weight $h_i/\mu_i=-\log\mu_i/S$ decreases with market size. It is tilted toward smaller firms relative to capitalization weights, but it is not equal weighted. A very small firm's absolute contribution $-\mu_i\log\mu_i$ still tends to zero as its weight tends to zero.

Ito's formula applied to $S(\mu)$ and then to $\log S(\mu)$ gives the master identity. In integrated form, with wealth normalized consistently,

$$
\log\frac{Z_h(t)/Z_h(0)}{Z_\mu(t)/Z_\mu(0)}
=\log\frac{S(t)}{S(0)}
+\int_0^t\frac{\gamma_\mu^*(s)}{S(s)}ds.
$$

This exact decomposition eliminates expected-return forecasts. The first term measures the change in market entropy; it can be negative. The second accumulates cross-sectional variation and is nonnegative. Consequently, finite-horizon relative performance need not be positive merely because the instantaneous excess-growth rate is positive.

If $S(t)\ge s_*>0$ and $\gamma_\mu^*(t)\ge c_*>0$, then

$$
\log\frac{Z_h(t)/Z_h(0)}{Z_\mu(t)/Z_\mu(0)}
\ge\log\frac{s_*}{S(0)}+\frac{c_*t}{\log n}.
$$

This explicit bound explains the eventual domination in the dividend-free price-wealth comparison. The guarantee depends on diversity and nondegeneracy holding over the whole required horizon, not on observing a diverse market on one date.

## 10. Dividends change the comparison

Let $d_i$ be the dividend rate, $\rho_i=g_i+d_i$ the total log-growth rate, and $d_\pi=\sum_i\pi_i d_i$. The total-return value is

$$
\widetilde Z_\pi(t)=Z_\pi(t)\exp\left(\int_0^t d_\pi(s)ds\right).
$$

This reinvests dividends across the portfolio in its current proportions. It differs from separately reinvesting each firm's dividend only in that firm and then summing those accumulated total-return values.

The total-return version of the master identity is

$$
\log\frac{\widetilde Z_h(t)/\widetilde Z_h(0)}
{\widetilde Z_\mu(t)/\widetilde Z_\mu(0)}
=\log\frac{S(t)}{S(0)}+
\int_0^t\left[d_h-d_\mu+\frac{\gamma_\mu^*}{S}\right](s)ds.
$$

Thus the dividend differential can offset the apparent price-only entropy advantage. Applying the price-only conclusion directly to dividend-reinvested market returns would omit an economically central term.

Under equal total log-growth rates and nonnegative dividends, Proposition 3.3 proves

$$
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac1t\int_0^t
(d_\mu(s)-\gamma_\mu^*(s))ds\ge0
\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$

A diverse market under those assumptions therefore needs a positive long-run market dividend rate. This is a necessary implication of the model, not a sufficient construction of equilibrium or a proof that any dividend policy preserves diversity.

## 11. Weak equilibrium and its precise probability statement

The paper defines domination as strictly greater normalized **total-return** wealth almost surely at some deterministic positive horizon. Weak equilibrium means that no portfolio dominates the market in that sense. It is weaker than a complete competitive equilibrium model and should not be identified without further argument with every modern no-arbitrage condition.

Theorem 5.1 says that for every $\epsilon>0$, sufficiently large deterministic $t$ satisfies

$$
\Pr\left\{
\frac1t\int_0^t(d_\mu-d_h)ds+\epsilon
>\frac1t\int_0^t\frac{\gamma_\mu^*}{S}ds
\right\}>0.
$$

The **positive probability** qualifier is crucial. The theorem does not assert this inequality almost surely for all large times, nor does it require every larger company to pay a higher dividend rate than every smaller company at every instant. It says the market-weighted dividend advantage must be capable of offsetting the entropy portfolio's accumulated benefit, enough to avoid certain domination. A corollary rules out identical dividend rates for all stocks in a diverse weakly equilibrated market.

The final CAPM example adds an “entropic equilibrium” assumption that $\log S(t)$ is a martingale and an instantaneous reward-to-risk comparison. It then obtains a more quantitative lower bound on $d_\mu-d_h$ involving $\gamma_\mu^*/S$, portfolio variances, their volatility ratio, and the market risk premium. These extra assumptions are not part of the earlier pathwise identity.

There is no empirical backtest or estimated dividend-size relation in this article. Its achievement is an exact connection between capitalization diversity, relative variation, an explicitly constructed portfolio, and necessary dividend conditions within a stylized market. Entry, share issuance, repurchases, corporate restructuring, jumps, and trading frictions can be alternative or additional mechanisms in real markets, but their effects require an extended model.
