# 1. Metadata

- **Title:** Continuous-Time Mean-Variance Portfolio Selection: A Stochastic LQ Framework
- **Author(s):** X. Y. Zhou, D. Li
- **Year:** 2000
- **Journal/Venue:** *Applied Mathematics and Optimization*

# 2. Problem statement

The paper solves the continuous-time analogue of Markowitz portfolio choice in a frictionless market with one bond and $m$ risky assets. The precise problem is:
$$
\min \operatorname{Var}[X(T)] \quad \text{subject to} \quad E[X(T)]=z,
$$
where $X(T)$ is terminal wealth generated by self-financing trading over $[0,T]$. Equivalently, for a scalarization parameter $\mu>0$, the paper studies
$$
\min_{u(\cdot)} \bigl\{\mu\,\operatorname{Var}[X(T)]-E[X(T)]\bigr\}.
$$
The technical obstacle is that the variance term contains $(E[X(T)])^2$, which is nonseparable and breaks standard dynamic programming.

# 3. Approach (short)

The method is stochastic control, specifically linear-quadratic (LQ) control. Zhou and Li embed the nonstandard mean-variance problem into an auxiliary family of standard stochastic LQ problems with linear terminal reward and quadratic terminal penalty. They then solve the auxiliary problem by a Riccati equation, recover the optimal feedback rule, and eliminate the auxiliary multiplier to obtain a closed-form efficient frontier. The novelty is not LQ control itself, but the embedding argument that converts a time-inconsistent variance objective into a tractable LQ family.

# 4. Approach (detailed)

1. **Market model and admissible strategies**

   Let the bond satisfy
   $$
   dS_0(t)=r(t)S_0(t)\,dt,
   $$
   and the risky assets satisfy
   $$
   dS_i(t)=S_i(t)\Bigl(b_i(t)\,dt+\sum_{j=1}^m \sigma_{ij}(t)\,dW_j(t)\Bigr),\qquad i=1,\dots,m.
   $$
   If $u_i(t)$ is the money invested in risky asset $i$, with $u(t)\in\mathbb R^m$, then wealth evolves as
   $$
   dX(t)=\bigl(r(t)X(t)+B(t)u(t)\bigr)\,dt+u(t)^\top \sigma(t)\,dW(t),
   $$
   where
   $$
   B(t)=\bigl(b_1(t)-r(t),\dots,b_m(t)-r(t)\bigr).
   $$
   The paper assumes $\sigma(t)\sigma(t)^\top$ is uniformly positive definite, so the risk premia can be translated into a well-defined instantaneous squared Sharpe ratio
   $$
   \rho(t)=B(t)\,[\sigma(t)\sigma(t)^\top]^{-1}B(t)^\top.
   $$

2. **Scalarized mean-variance problem**

   For each $\mu>0$, define
   $$
   P(\mu):\qquad \min_{u(\cdot)}\ \mu \operatorname{Var}[X(T)]-E[X(T)].
   $$
   Since
   $$
   \operatorname{Var}[X(T)]=E[X(T)^2]-(E[X(T)])^2,
   $$
   the term $(E[X(T)])^2$ is the source of nonseparability. Standard Bellman recursion does not apply directly because the cost-to-go cannot be written as conditional expectation of a function of the current state alone.

3. **Embedding into an auxiliary LQ problem**

   The key move is to introduce, for $(\mu,\lambda)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb R$,
   $$
   A(\mu,\lambda):\qquad \min_{u(\cdot)} E\bigl[\mu X(T)^2-\lambda X(T)\bigr].
   $$
   This is now a standard stochastic LQ terminal-cost problem. Theorem 3.1 proves:
   $$
   \mathcal P(\mu)\subseteq \bigcup_{\lambda\in\mathbb R}\mathcal A(\mu,\lambda),
   $$
   and more sharply, if $\bar u$ solves $P(\mu)$, then it solves $A(\mu,\bar\lambda)$ for
   $$
   \bar\lambda=1+2\mu E[\bar X(T)].
   $$

   The proof is the conceptual core of the paper. Define
   $$
   \pi(x,y)=\mu x-\mu y^2-y.
   $$
   Since $\pi$ is concave in $(x,y)$, and since
   $$
   \pi(E[X(T)^2],E[X(T)])=\mu \operatorname{Var}[X(T)]-E[X(T)],
   $$
   any control that failed to solve the auxiliary problem at $\lambda=1+2\mu E[\bar X(T)]$ would improve the scalarized mean-variance objective by the first-order upper support inequality for a concave function. This converts the time-inconsistent problem into a parameterized family of time-consistent problems.

4. **General stochastic LQ solution**

   For a generic stochastic LQ problem, the value function is quadratic in the state,
   $$
   V(t,x)=P(t)x^2+2g(t)x+h(t),
   $$
   with $P$ and $g$ determined by a Riccati ODE and a linear backward ODE. In the mean-variance specialization, the running control penalty matrix is singular:
   $$
   R(t)=0.
   $$
   The paper emphasizes that this does not destroy solvability because the effective convexity comes from the diffusion term through $\sigma\sigma^\top$, not from an explicit $u^\top R u$ term.

5. **Specialization to the portfolio problem**

   For the auxiliary problem, the Riccati equation collapses to the scalar ODE
   $$
   \dot P(t)=(\rho(t)-2r(t))P(t),\qquad P(T)=\mu,
   $$
   with solution
   $$
   P(t)=\mu \exp\!\Bigl(-\int_t^T (\rho(s)-2r(s))\,ds\Bigr).
   $$
   The linear term satisfies
   $$
   \dot g(t)=(\rho(t)-r(t))g(t)-\gamma r(t)P(t),\qquad g(T)=0,
   $$
   where $\gamma=\lambda/(2\mu)$. Solving for $g/P$ gives
   $$
   \frac{g(t)}{P(t)}=\gamma\Bigl(1-e^{-\int_t^T r(s)\,ds}\Bigr).
   $$
   Substituting into the LQ feedback rule yields the optimal dollar position:
   $$
   u^*(t,x)=\bigl[\sigma(t)\sigma(t)^\top\bigr]^{-1}B(t)^\top
   \Bigl(\gamma e^{-\int_t^T r(s)\,ds}-x\Bigr).
   $$
   The policy is affine in current wealth. The investor holds more risky exposure when current wealth is below the discounted target level $\gamma e^{-\int_t^T r}$, and de-risks when wealth is above it.

6. **Recovering the efficient frontier**

   Under the optimal feedback, the moments of $X(T)$ satisfy closed-form ODEs. Solving them yields
   $$
   E[X(T)]=\alpha x_0+\beta\gamma,\qquad E[X(T)^2]=\delta x_0^2+\beta\gamma^2,
   $$
   with
   $$
   \alpha=\exp\!\Bigl(\int_0^T (r(t)-\rho(t))\,dt\Bigr),\qquad
   \beta=1-\exp\!\Bigl(-\int_0^T \rho(t)\,dt\Bigr),\qquad
   \delta=\exp\!\Bigl(\int_0^T (2r(t)-\rho(t))\,dt\Bigr).
   $$
   Eliminating $\gamma$ gives Theorem 6.1:
   $$
   \operatorname{Var}[X(T)]
   =
   \frac{e^{-\int_0^T \rho(t)\,dt}}{1-e^{-\int_0^T \rho(t)\,dt}}
   \Bigl(E[X(T)]-x_0 e^{\int_0^T r(t)\,dt}\Bigr)^2.
   $$
   Hence in mean-standard-deviation space,
   $$
   E[X(T)]
   =
   x_0 e^{\int_0^T r(t)\,dt}
   +
   \sqrt{\frac{1-e^{-\int_0^T \rho(t)\,dt}}{e^{-\int_0^T \rho(t)\,dt}}}\;
   \sigma_{X(T)},
   $$
   a straight line. This is the continuous-time capital market line.

7. **What is exact and what is not**

   - The embedding theorem and the Riccati solution are exact under the model assumptions.
   - The frontier formula is exact.
   - No approximation is used in deriving the feedback rule or efficient frontier.
   - The baseline requires deterministic bounded coefficients, uniformly nondegenerate risky covariance, square-integrable controls, frictionless continuous trading, and unrestricted dollar positions; these are substantive modeling restrictions.

# 5. Domain of applicability

- The method applies to continuous-time self-financing portfolio choice with quadratic terminal risk and linear terminal reward.
- It relies on linear wealth dynamics and diffusion risk, so it does **not** directly cover proportional transaction costs, jump-diffusions, state constraints, drawdown constraints, or intermediate consumption.
- The coefficients are deterministic in this paper. Later work extends the framework, but the closed-form frontier here is proved only in that regime.
- The straight-line frontier depends on the presence of the risk-free asset. Without the bond, the frontier need not be a perfect square and the capital-market-line interpretation breaks.
- The paper’s genuinely novel point is the embedding theorem: the LQ machinery is known, but the proof that the mean-variance problem can be solved through an auxiliary LQ family is what makes the continuous-time Markowitz problem analytically tractable.

## 6. Solving directly for a specified expected terminal wealth

Let $R=\int_0^T r(t)dt$, $H=\int_0^T\rho(t)dt$, and suppose $H>0$. For a prescribed target $z=E[X_T]$, the moment equation gives the auxiliary terminal target

$$
\gamma_z=\frac{z-x_0e^{R-H}}{1-e^{-H}}.
$$

The trading rule is then

$$
u_t=(\sigma_t\sigma_t^\top)^{-1}B_t^\top
\left(\gamma_z e^{-\int_t^T r_sds}-X_t\right).
$$

The distinction between $z$ and $\gamma_z$ matters. The former is the desired expected terminal wealth; the latter is the larger quadratic-loss target used by the embedded control problem. Setting $\gamma=z$ would generally produce the wrong expected return. For an efficient target above the risk-free terminal wealth, $z>x_0e^R$, one has $\gamma_z>z$.

For the scalarized problem $\mu\operatorname{Var}(X_T)-E[X_T]$, the fixed-point relation implies

$$
\gamma=x_0e^R+\frac{e^H}{2\mu},\qquad
E[X_T]=x_0e^R+\frac{e^H-1}{2\mu},
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_T)=\frac{e^H-1}{4\mu^2}.
$$

These formulas make the calibration of risk aversion transparent. Increasing $\mu$ lowers the excess terminal mean in inverse proportion and terminal variance in inverse-square proportion. They also check the factor-of-two convention in the scalar objective; a convention using $\mu/2$ before variance produces different parameter labels, although the same frontier.

## 7. An alternative derivation through the target gap

Define the discounted target $K_t=\gamma e^{-\int_t^Tr_sds}$ and the gap $Y_t=K_t-X_t$. Since $dK_t=r_tK_tdt$, substituting the feedback rule into the wealth equation yields

$$
dY_t=(r_t-\rho_t)Y_tdt-Y_t\theta_t^\top dW_t,
\quad\theta_t=\sigma_t^\top(\sigma_t\sigma_t^\top)^{-1}B_t^\top,
\quad\|\theta_t\|^2=\rho_t.
$$

The gap is a geometric diffusion:

$$
Y_t=Y_0\exp\left\{\int_0^t(r_s-\tfrac32\rho_s)ds
-\int_0^t\theta_s^\top dW_s\right\}.
$$

For an efficient positive-premium target, $Y_0>0$, hence the gap stays positive. The wealth process remains below the moving target $K_t$, but it need not remain nonnegative: $X_t=K_t-Y_t$, and the positive gap can become arbitrarily large. This is why the model should not be described as a capital-preservation guarantee. Square-integrability of the control is the paper's admissibility condition; a no-bankruptcy or margin constraint is an additional restriction.

Taking moments of the gap gives another check on the efficient frontier. At maturity,

$$
E[Y_T]=(\gamma-x_0e^R)e^{-H},\qquad
E[Y_T^2]=(\gamma-x_0e^R)^2e^{-H}.
$$

Consequently

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_T)=\operatorname{Var}(Y_T)
=(\gamma-x_0e^R)^2e^{-H}(1-e^{-H}).
$$

Eliminating $\gamma$ recovers $(z-x_0e^R)^2/(e^H-1)$. This calculation is an explanatory derivation of the source's moment formulas, not a separate theorem about constrained wealth processes.

## 8. Degenerate cases and the meaning of the frontier

Uniform nondegeneracy of $\sigma\sigma^\top$ guarantees that risk is well defined in every risky direction. It does not guarantee a nonzero market price of risk: $B_t$ may vanish. If $H=0$, the displayed frontier with denominator $1-e^{-H}$ cannot be used. There is no positive expected excess return to exploit in this deterministic-coefficient model; the all-bond allocation is the efficient point. An implementation must branch on this case rather than divide by zero.

The variance relation contains two algebraic branches when one solves for expected wealth. Mean-variance efficiency uses the upper branch $z\ge x_0e^R$. A target below the all-bond outcome can be included in a constrained mean-target problem, but is dominated in the original bicriteria problem by the riskless allocation.

The slope $\sqrt{e^H-1}$ belongs to a terminal-wealth frontier generated by adaptive trading. It should not be substituted uncritically for a one-period or instantaneous Sharpe ratio. For small $H$, it is approximately $\sqrt H$; over longer horizons, the dynamic feedback and compounding are economically important. Different deterministic coefficient paths with the same integrated $r$ and $\rho$ give the same terminal frontier, although the actual positions and intermediate risks differ.

## 9. The paper's numerical illustration

The example uses a bond rate of 6%, stock drift of 12%, stock volatility of 15%, and a one-year horizon. The instantaneous squared Sharpe ratio is $0.16$, so the frontier is

$$
E[X_1]=1.06184x_0+0.41655\,\operatorname{sd}(X_1).
$$

For initial wealth of one million dollars and a target expected terminal wealth of 1.2 million, the required terminal standard deviation is approximately 0.3317 million. The embedded target is $\gamma\approx1.9963$ million. The initial risky holding is approximately 2.3468 million, financed by borrowing 1.3468 million in the bond. The source deliberately uses this example to expose the aggressive leverage required by the return target.

The 33.17% figure is terminal standard deviation relative to initial wealth. It is not the volatility of the stock, and it is not a maximum drawdown or loss bound. This example is a model calculation; the paper does not supply an out-of-sample trading experiment establishing the profitability of the policy under estimated coefficients.

## 10. Pre-commitment, implementation, and model risk

The solution optimizes an objective fixed at the initial date. The auxiliary problem is dynamically tractable because its terminal loss is an expectation of a quadratic, but this does not turn the original variance objective into a time-consistent preference. Re-solving the original mean-variance problem at an intermediate date usually changes the effective target and therefore changes the original policy. This distinction separates the Zhou-Li pre-commitment frontier from later equilibrium or time-consistent mean-variance strategies.

To implement the model mechanically, estimate deterministic paths for the risk-free rate, excess drifts and covariance; compute $H$ and the desired $\gamma$; solve linear systems for $(\sigma\sigma^\top)^{-1}B^\top$; and rebalance the dollar exposure using current wealth. At every date the riskless dollar holding is the residual $X_t-\mathbf1^\top u_t$. Treating $u$ as portfolio percentages is a units error. A discretized implementation should verify empirical terminal first and second moments against the formulas before introducing real-world constraints.

Drift estimation is a material weakness: the same estimated premia enter both the risky direction and the squared Sharpe integral that determines the target. Transaction costs, asymmetric funding rates, leverage limits and insolvency rules change the attainable set. Clipping the theoretical feedback rule may be a useful practical heuristic, but it no longer has the stated efficient-frontier guarantee. The value of the paper is a solvable analytical benchmark and a route into stochastic LQ methods, with the implementation gap made explicit.
