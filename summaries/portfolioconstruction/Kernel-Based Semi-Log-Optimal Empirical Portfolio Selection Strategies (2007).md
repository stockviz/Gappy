# 1. Metadata

- **Title:** Kernel-Based Semi-Log-Optimal Empirical Portfolio Selection Strategies
- **Author(s):** László Györfi, András Urbán, István Vajda
- **Year:** 2007
- **Journal/Venue:** *International Journal of Theoretical and Applied Finance*

# 2. Problem statement

The paper studies sequential investment under minimal distributional assumptions. The precise question is: **can one construct a computationally lighter approximation to the kernel-based log-optimal strategy that admits an explicit asymptotic growth-loss bound relative to the log-optimal strategy?**

# 3. Approach (short)

The method is nonparametric sequential portfolio selection under ergodicity. The authors replace the full conditional log-optimal portfolio problem by a semi-log approximation that depends only on first and second conditional moments, estimate those moments by kernel matching of past return windows, and aggregate a countable family of experts. The proof adapts the earlier kernel-log-optimal argument and retains an explicit third-order approximation loss.

# 4. Approach (detailed)

1. **Market model and growth criterion**

   Let $X_n\in\mathbb R_+^d$ be the price-relative vector, assumed only stationary and ergodic. A constant rebalanced portfolio $b\in\Delta_d$ yields wealth
   $$
   S_n(b)=\prod_{i=1}^n \langle b,X_i\rangle.
   $$
   The full-information optimal asymptotic growth rate is
   $$
   W^\star
   =
   \mathbb E\!\left[
   \max_{b\in\Delta_d}
   \mathbb E\big(\log\langle b,X_0\rangle \mid X_{-\infty}^{-1}\big)
   \right].
   $$

2. **Semi-log objective**

   Instead of maximizing $\log \langle b,X\rangle$, the paper uses a quadratic approximation. Up to irrelevant constants this takes the form
   $$
   \Psi(b)
   =
   \mathbb E\big[\langle b,X\rangle-1\mid \mathcal F\big]
   -
   \frac12
   \mathbb E\big[(\langle b,X\rangle-1)^2\mid \mathcal F\big],
   $$
   where $\mathcal F$ denotes the predictive sigma-field generated by the past. This is an approximation, not an exact reformulation.

3. **Build kernel experts**

   For window length $k$ and radius parameter $\ell$, define the match set
   $$
   J_n=\{i: \|X_{i-k}^{i-1}-X_{n-k}^{n-1}\|\le r_{k,\ell}\}.
   $$
   The expert $h^{(k,\ell)}$ chooses
   $$
   h^{(k,\ell)}(X_1^{n-1})
   \in
   \arg\max_{b\in\Delta_d}
   \sum_{i\in J_n}
   \left[
   \langle b,X_i\rangle-1
   -
   \frac12(\langle b,X_i\rangle-1)^2
   \right].
   $$
   This replaces the costly kernel-log problem by a moment-based optimization.

4. **Aggregate experts**

   Assign positive prior weights $q_{k,\ell}$ to the countable family of experts and define aggregate wealth by mixture:
   $$
   S_n(B)=\sum_{k,\ell} q_{k,\ell} S_n(H^{(k,\ell)}).
   $$
   The actual portfolio is the normalized wealth-weighted average of expert portfolios, as in standard prediction-with-experts constructions.

5. **Main theorem**

   Theorem 4.1 states an asymptotic lower bound with a third-order shortfall:
   $$
   \liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log S_n(B)\ge W^\star-\frac56 E[\max_j|X^{(j)}-1|^3]
   \quad\text{a.s.}
   $$
   under stationarity, ergodicity, $X^{(j)}\ge0.6$, and finite third moments. The nonzero remainder prevents inference of exact equality with $W^\star$.

6. **Proof sketch**

   The proof is explicitly described as a modification of the earlier kernel-log-optimal proof. The main ingredients are:
   - Breiman’s generalized ergodic theorem to replace empirical averages on matched windows by conditional expectations;
   - a continuity/selection lemma ensuring maximizers of the semi-log criterion behave well under weak convergence of empirical conditional laws;
   - mixture-expert lower bounds showing the aggregate performs asymptotically as well as the best expert sequence.

   Once the kernel estimates of conditional first and second moments converge, the semi-log objective converges to its conditional-population counterpart. The expert family is rich enough that some $(k,\ell)$-sequence approximates the infinite-past predictor arbitrarily well.

7. **Exact versus approximate claims**

   The semi-log objective is only an approximation to $\log\langle b,X\rangle$. What is exact is the growth-shortfall bound under the stated assumptions. The induced strategy need not achieve precisely the log-optimal growth rate.

# 5. Domain of applicability

The strategy applies to sequential investment in stationary ergodic markets when the investor is willing to assume enough regularity for nonparametric conditioning to work and is mainly concerned with asymptotic growth. It is attractive when exact kernel log-optimal optimization is computationally burdensome. The limitations are strong: no transaction costs, no short selling, dependence on tuning sequences $r_{k,\ell}$, and asymptotic rather than finite-sample guarantees. The stationarity/ergodicity assumption is also doing most of the heavy lifting; the paper does not justify it empirically.

## 6. The actual theorem is a bounded growth shortfall

The source is *International Journal of Theoretical and Applied Finance* 10(3), 505-516. Theorem 4.1 assumes a stationary ergodic market, each price relative bounded below by 0.6, and finite third absolute moments of deviations from one. It gives

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log S_n(\bar B)
\ge W^* -\frac56E\left[\max_j|X^{(j)}-1|^3\right]
\quad\text{a.s.}
$$

The cubic term does not vanish merely because the sample grows. Thus the paper proves an asymptotic **near-optimality bound**, not exact universal consistency with the log-optimal growth rate. Exact consistency belongs to the earlier full-log kernel method discussed for comparison. This correction changes the central theoretical claim of the previous summary.

The lower bound on price relatives controls the remainder near the logarithm's singularity. For $z\ge0.6$, the proof uses

$$
h(z)-\tfrac12|z-1|^3\le\log z\le h(z)+\tfrac13|z-1|^3,
\quad h(z)=z-1-\tfrac12(z-1)^2.
$$

Comparing a quadratic optimizer with a log optimizer incurs the sum of the two remainder constants, $1/2+1/3=5/6$. Because a long-only portfolio relative is a convex combination of asset relatives,

$$
|b^\top X-1|^3\le\sum_jb_j|X_j-1|^3\le\max_j|X_j-1|^3.
$$

This converts the scalar Taylor bound into a uniform portfolio bound. It also explains why the guarantee becomes less informative for jumpy assets or large universe-wide extremes.

## 7. Moment compression and the computational saving

For each context length $k$ and radius $r_{k,\ell}$, consider only historical successors $X_i$ whose immediately preceding $k$ vectors match the current context. The causality restriction is $k<i<n$; $X_n$ is not available when selecting date-$n$ weights. With no matches, the method uses equal weights.

Set $Y_i=X_i-\mathbf1$ and precompute

$$
m_J=\sum_{i\in J}Y_i,\qquad C_J=\frac12\sum_{i\in J}Y_iY_i^\top.
$$

Then the expert solves the concave quadratic program

$$
\max_{b\ge0,\,\mathbf1^\top b=1}\ b^\top m_J-b^\top C_Jb.
$$

$C_J$ is a raw second-moment matrix of simple returns, not their centered covariance. Replacing it by a covariance matrix drops the squared mean term and creates another approximation beyond the paper's semi-log criterion. Once $m_J$ and $C_J$ are assembled, evaluating the objective and its gradient no longer requires traversing all matches. Searching for matches still incurs a history- and dimension-dependent cost; the paper does not eliminate that part of the computation.

The mixture is implemented by allocating date-$n$ capital to each expert in proportion to $q_{k,\ell}S_{n-1}(H^{(k,\ell)})$. This is a portfolio average with weights based on prior wealth, not an arithmetic average of current expert log returns. The identity $S_n=\sum qS_n(H)$ implies $\log S_n\ge\log q_{k,\ell}+\log S_n(H^{(k,\ell)})$ for every fixed expert. The per-period prior penalty disappears asymptotically, providing the link from each local expert's limit to the aggregate bound.

## 8. Numerical evidence and its interpretation

The experiments use the standard 36-stock NYSE dataset with 5,651 trading days over 22 years ending in 1985. The theoretical countable family is truncated to $K=5$ context lengths and $L=10$ radii, with uniform prior weight across 50 experts. The radius specification is $r_{k,\ell}^2=0.0001\,d k\ell$.

For IBM/Coca-Cola, terminal wealth from one unit is reported as 52.3 for the full-log kernel rule and 52.2 for semi-log, versus 15.02 for the best constant rebalanced portfolio. For Commercial Metals/Mei Corp, the corresponding values are 1,224 and 1,219, versus 103 for the hindsight constant portfolio. Other pairs produce spectacular gross values, and the table shows nearly identical semi-log and full-log outcomes. The experiment supports computational substitution with little loss on this dataset; it does not establish investable net wealth at those levels.

The “oracle” chooses the best asset after seeing each day's outcome, and the best expert and best constant portfolio are also identified in hindsight. They are comparison benchmarks, not causal strategies. The implemented mixture is causal. The experiment excludes commissions, impact, market capacity and constraints on frequent reallocations; the huge wealth figures make those omissions economically decisive.

## 9. Practical boundaries

A finite 50-expert implementation does not inherit the full infinite-family theorem without additional approximation analysis. Stationarity and ergodicity allow dependence and do not supply useful finite-sample convergence rates. As context dimension $dk$ grows, matches can become sparse, making the asymptotic guarantee a poor guide to practical data requirements.

A reproduction should verify that every match uses only past successors, solve with true raw second moments, compute mixtures in log scale to avoid numerical overflow, and compare full-log versus quadratic decisions under identical experts. Report cubic remainder diagnostics, turnover, and sensitivity to the lower-tail support condition. The result is most useful when individual-period returns are small enough that the cubic term is modest and repeated log optimization is computationally expensive.
