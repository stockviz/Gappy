## 1. Metadata

- **Author(s):** Robert M. Anderson, Stephen W. Bianchi, Lisa R. Goldberg
- **Year:** 2014
- **Journal/Venue:** Forthcoming in *Financial Analysts Journal* at the time of the manuscript

## 2. Problem statement

The paper asks how leverage affects realized portfolio performance over multiple periods. Specifically: **what determines the arithmetic and geometric return of a levered strategy built from a fully invested source portfolio, and what role is played by the covariance between leverage and the source portfolio’s excess return?**

## 3. Approach (short)

The paper derives exact arithmetic-return decompositions for levered strategies and then combines them with a geometric-return approximation to account for compounding. The novel term is a covariance correction between leverage and the source portfolio’s excess borrowing return. This term is especially important for dynamically levered, volatility-targeting strategies, because leverage moves inversely with estimated volatility and often covaries negatively with subsequent excess returns.

## 4. Approach (detailed)

1. **Single-period levered return**

   Let $r^S$ be the return on the fully invested source portfolio, $r^b$ the borrowing rate, and $\lambda$ the leverage ratio. The single-period return to the levered strategy is
   $$
   r^L=\lambda r^S-(\lambda-1)r^b.
   $$
   Excess return relative to the risk-free rate $r^f$ is
   $$
   r^L-r^f
   =
   \lambda(r^S-r^f)-(\lambda-1)(r^b-r^f).
   $$
   If $r^b>r^f$, Sharpe ratio declines with leverage even in a one-period setting.

2. **Arithmetic-return decomposition**

   Rewriting,
   $$
   r^L=r^S+(\lambda-1)(r^S-r^b).
   $$
   Taking expectations over time yields
   $$
   E[r^L]
   =
   E[r^S]
   +
   E[\lambda-1]\,E[r^S-r^b]
   +
   \operatorname{cov}(\lambda,r^S-r^b).
   $$
   This is the paper’s central formula. The first two terms are the **magnified source return**. The third is the newly emphasized covariance term.

3. **Adding trading costs**

   With turnover-induced trading costs,
   $$
   E[r^L]
   =
   E[r^S]
   +
   E[\lambda-1]E[r^S-r^b]
   +
   \operatorname{cov}(\lambda,r^S-r^b)
   -
   E[r_{TC}^S+r_{TC}^L].
   $$
   The paper separates trading cost due to turnover in the source portfolio from trading cost generated by leverage rebalancing itself.

4. **Why the covariance term appears**

   In a fixed-leverage strategy, $\lambda$ is constant, so
   $$
   \operatorname{cov}(\lambda,r^S-r^b)=0.
   $$
   In a dynamic-leverage strategy, especially volatility targeting, $\lambda_t$ varies over time. Since leverage is often increased after low-volatility periods and reduced after high-volatility periods, it can covary negatively with the source portfolio’s subsequent excess return.

   The paper’s two-period example makes this exact. When leverage rises before a bad return and falls before a good one, arithmetic mean return is reduced by the negative covariance term even if average leverage looks reasonable.

5. **Compounding and variance drag**

   Arithmetic expected return is not the full story. For geometric return the paper uses the approximation
   $$
   G[r]\approx (1+E[r])e^{-\operatorname{var}(r)/2}-1.
   $$
   Thus compounding imposes a variance drag, and leverage magnifies that drag because strategy variance rises with leverage. Dynamic leverage can therefore hurt performance through two distinct channels:

   - variance drag;
   - covariance drag.

6. **Fixed vs dynamic leverage**

   The decomposition clarifies an important distinction:

   - **fixed leverage:** no covariance drag, but still financing cost and variance drag;
   - **dynamic leverage / volatility targeting:** all of the above, plus covariance drag.

   This is why a volatility-targeted strategy can underperform a fixed-leverage strategy even if both target similar long-run volatility.

7. **Empirical implication**

   In the historical examples studied by the paper, the covariance term is negative over long horizons for several volatility-targeting strategies, substantially lowering realized return and Sharpe ratio. The point is not that the covariance must always be negative, but that it is economically large enough that ignoring it is a material error.

8. **Proof logic**

   The arithmetic attribution is exact algebra, not asymptotics; the compounding adjustment is approximate. Starting from
   $$
   r^L=r^S+(\lambda-1)(r^S-r^b),
   $$
   use the identity
   $$
   E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]+\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)
   $$
   with $X=\lambda-1$, $Y=r^S-r^b$. The geometric-return approximation is the standard lognormal-style approximation based on the effect of volatility on compound growth.

**Additional mathematical details**

The paper’s exact arithmetic decomposition is
$$
r_t^L
=
r_t^S+(\lambda_t-1)(r_t^S-r_t^b),
$$
so averaging over time gives
$$
E[r^L]
=
E[r^S]
+E[\lambda-1]\,E[r^S-r^b]
+\operatorname{cov}(\lambda,r^S-r^b).
$$
The covariance term is therefore not a higher-order approximation; it is an exact component of average arithmetic performance whenever leverage varies through time.

The geometric-return analysis is the approximate part. Using the usual log-return expansion,
$$
g \approx E[r]-\frac12 \operatorname{Var}(r),
$$
the paper shows that dynamic leverage can lower compound growth through two distinct channels: variance drag and covariance drag. Fixed leverage eliminates the second term mechanically because $\lambda_t$ is constant; volatility targeting does not.

## 5. Domain of applicability

- The framework applies to **levered strategies built from a fully invested source portfolio**.
- It is especially relevant for **volatility-targeting** rules because those generate time-varying leverage.
- The arithmetic decomposition is exact; the geometric-return formula is an approximation.
- The paper does not prove that covariance drag is always negative. That sign is empirical and strategy dependent.
- Its strongest contribution is analytical: any serious evaluation of levered portfolio performance must account for leverage-return covariance, not just average leverage.


## 6. Five components, and an exact finite-sample identity

The local source is the April 21, 2014 manuscript, listed as forthcoming in *Financial Analysts Journal*. The abstract says 1929-2013, but the main empirical exhibits and their explicit captions use January 1929-December 2012, an 84-year interval. Numerical results below follow the table captions rather than silently reconciling that source inconsistency.

For a fully invested risky source portfolio, set $x_t=r^S_t-r^b_t$. Before trading costs,
$$
r^L_t=r^S_t+(\lambda_t-1)x_t.
$$
For $T$ observed periods, use means and a covariance with denominator **$T$**:
$$
\overline r^L=\overline r^S+(\overline\lambda-1)\overline x
+\frac1T\sum_t(\lambda_t-\overline\lambda)(x_t-\overline x).
$$
No probability model, stationarity, or asymptotic argument is needed for this retrospective identity. An unbiased sample covariance with denominator $T-1$ will not reconcile the attribution unless it is rescaled. Prospectively, the corresponding expectation identity requires the relevant moments to exist and estimates of the joint distribution of leverage and subsequent excess borrowing return.

The five economically distinct ingredients are source return; extra source return financed by average borrowing; leverage-return covariance; trading cost; and the compounding correction. Financing is already embedded through $x_t=r^S_t-r^b_t$, so a second subtraction of the same financing expense would double count it. Source returns in the attribution are gross of source trading costs; the later source-cost subtraction must use the same convention.

The covariance is economically important even when its correlation is small:
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\lambda,x)=\rho_{\lambda,x}\sigma_\lambda\sigma_x.
$$
A leverage rule that varies widely can create a material dollar-return contribution from a modest correlation. The sign is not dictated by the fact that leverage is inverse volatility. It depends on the joint relationship between the leverage available before the period and the return realized afterward. Positive and negative covariance terms both occur over shorter horizons in the source's experiments.

### A two-period reconciliation

The source asset returns +10% then -10%, with zero funding cost. Buy-and-hold source wealth goes from 100 to 110 to 99. A constant leverage of 2.5 produces +25% and -25%, leaving 93.75. Dynamic leverage of two before the first return and three before the second produces +20% and -30%, leaving 84. The two leveraged policies have identical average leverage but very different cumulative outcomes.

For the dynamic rule, $\operatorname{Cov}(\lambda,r^S)=-0.05$ per period. Its arithmetic average return is therefore -5%, while constant leverage has zero average return. Both happen to have return variance 0.0625 in this particular example. The remaining difference between arithmetic averages and compound wealth comes from compounding. The printed manuscript contains incidental decimal slips in the worked arithmetic; the reconciled wealth values are 99, 93.75, and 84, with losses of 1%, 6.25%, and 16%, respectively.

## 7. Constant leverage still requires trading

“Fixed leverage” does not mean buy-and-hold debt. If source assets rise, the investor's equity generally rises by more than the unlevered percentage, causing asset-to-equity leverage to fall. Restoring the original leverage requires buying more assets and borrowing more. After losses it requires selling. This mechanical rebalancing can incur cost even though $\lambda_t$ is constant and the leverage-return covariance term is exactly zero.

Appendix C works in equity and dollar holdings, with trading costs reducing the equity used in the new leverage ratio. Given current post-return assets and debt, choose the next risky-asset scale $a$ so that
$$
a=\lambda_{t+1}\left(L'_t-\kappa\|aw_{t+1}-A'_tw'_t\|_1\right),
$$
where $L'_t$ is equity before trading cost and $A'_tw'_t$ is the post-return risky holdings vector. The transaction cost depends on $a$, while $a$ itself depends on equity after that cost. The paper solves the resulting scalar equation by bisection in its examples. This is more careful than first setting a leverage target, subtracting costs afterward, and assuming the target still holds exactly.

The authors calculate source-portfolio trading costs separately and attribute the remaining cost to leverage. This residual attribution is an accounting partition; it does not identify an independent tradable “leverage strategy.” Extremely low remaining equity can make the desired rebalance unaffordable. The examples do not encounter such a failure, but the appendix recognizes that it is possible.

## 8. Compounding: exact measurement and a useful approximation

For strictly positive gross returns,
$$
G=\left(\prod_t(1+r_t)\right)^{1/T}-1
=\exp\left(\frac1T\sum_t\log(1+r_t)\right)-1.
$$
This exact statistic ranks terminal wealth over equal horizons. The source's approximation follows two second-order expansions:
$$
\overline{\log(1+r)}\simeq\overline r-\frac12\overline{r^2}
\simeq\log(1+\overline r)-\frac12\operatorname{Var}(r),
$$
and hence $G\simeq(1+\overline r)e^{-\operatorname{Var}(r)/2}-1$. It is not an exact formula for arbitrary simple-return distributions or a consequence of lognormality alone. Large negative returns and higher moments can make the neglected terms material. In the paper's reported examples, the annual approximation error is at most roughly one basis point for parity strategies and five basis points for levered bonds.

Annualization is another source of apparent contradictions. Arithmetic monthly means are multiplied by 12; geometric monthly returns are compounded as $(1+G)^{12}-1$. A low-volatility portfolio can therefore have an annualized geometric figure slightly above twelve times its monthly arithmetic mean. That does not violate Jensen's inequality, which compares measures at the same frequency with consistent compounding conventions.

## 9. Empirical attribution and benchmark design

The paper studies unlevered stock/bond parity and Treasury bonds as source portfolios. It compares unconditional volatility targets, conditional targets based on benchmark volatility, and fixed-leverage policies matched either on average leverage or on realized risk. A fixed target selected from the full evaluation sample is a hindsight-normalized experiment; a contemporaneous trailing-volatility rule can be implemented sequentially. Their target definitions should not be mixed.

In the central parity example, Table 2 reports gross source arithmetic return 5.75% annually and an extra borrowing-based magnification of 3.97%, giving idealized magnified return 9.72%. The leverage-return covariance subtracts 1.84 points, source trading subtracts 0.07, and leverage-induced trading subtracts 0.96. Net arithmetic return is 6.85%; the compounding calculation gives geometric return 6.37%. Table 2's row labelled “Leverage” is $E[\lambda-1]=2.66$, whereas Table 3's average total leverage is $E[\lambda]=3.66$. Confusing these rows changes the inferred exposure by a full unit of equity.

The 60/40 benchmark's geometric return is 7.77%, volatility 11.58%, and Sharpe 0.40. The targeted parity strategy has geometric return 6.37%, volatility 11.54%, and Sharpe 0.29. Its unlevered source has geometric return 5.74%, volatility 4.20%, and Sharpe 0.52. Leverage raises source growth only modestly after the other components while greatly increasing risk in this calibration.

Across the volatility-targeting examples, the long-sample covariance term subtracts approximately 0.64-4.23 percentage points per year. The largest cited effect arises when low-volatility bonds are scaled toward stock-market volatility. Yet shorter intervals can have positive covariance; these estimates do not establish a universal negative return to volatility management.

Funding is proxied by three-month Eurodollar deposits from 1971 and T-bills plus 60 basis points earlier. Trading costs decline through historical eras. These are assumed implementation costs, and comparisons of levered with unlevered strategies are sensitive to them. Comparably levered benchmarks help distinguish a poor leverage *rule* from the common expense of financing any leveraged policy, although their turnover and exposure paths can still differ.

A useful research report should reconcile the five components in native period units, compare more than one funding model, distinguish hindsight normalization from deployable targets, and report the time variation of the covariance term. The paper's lasting contribution is this attribution discipline. It does not supply a universal optimal leverage rule or establish that fixed leverage must beat volatility targeting in other markets.
