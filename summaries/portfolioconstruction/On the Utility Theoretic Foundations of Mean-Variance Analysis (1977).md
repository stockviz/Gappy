## 1. Metadata

- **Title:** On the Utility Theoretic Foundations of Mean-Variance Analysis
- **Author(s):** David P. Baron
- **Year:** 1977
- **Journal/Venue:** *Journal of Finance*

## 2. Problem statement

The paper asks for the exact utility-theoretic conditions under which ranking risky alternatives solely by mean and variance is consistent with von Neumann-Morgenstern expected utility. Formally: for which utility functions $U$ and for which classes of return distributions does there exist an ordinal representation
$$
EU(X)=V(E[X],\operatorname{Var}(X))
$$
such that preference rankings induced by $V$ coincide with expected-utility rankings?

## 3. Approach (short)

This is a decision-theoretic characterization paper. Baron analyzes mixtures of lotteries under the NM axioms and studies when the induced indifference curves in $(\mu,\sigma^2)$-space can represent the full preference ordering. The paper belongs to expected-utility foundations and mean-variance theory, not to econometrics or optimization.

## 4. Approach (detailed)

1. **Set up the problem on the mixture space.**

   Let $X$ denote a return random variable generated by some action. Under the NM axioms there exists a utility index $U$ such that
   $$
   X_1 \succ X_2 \iff E[U(X_1)]>E[U(X_2)].
   $$
   Mean-variance analysis assumes this ranking can also be written as
   $$
   V(\mu,\sigma^2),\qquad \mu=E[X],\ \sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(X).
   $$

2. **Use indifference between two lotteries and their mixtures.**

   Suppose two lotteries with moments $(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ and $(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)$ are indifferent. A probability mixture $X_q$ chooses the first lottery with probability $q$ and the second otherwise; it is not the simultaneously held payoff $qX_1+(1-q)X_2$. Its moments are
   $$
   \mu_q=q\mu_1+(1-q)\mu_2,
   $$
   $$
   \sigma_q^2=q\sigma_1^2+(1-q)\sigma_2^2+q(1-q)(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2.
   $$
   The locus of all indifferent mixtures traces a parabola in $(\mu,\sigma^2)$-space.

3. **Derive the functional form implied by that parabola.**

   Because all such mixtures must remain indifferent under the NM mixture axiom, the quadratic expected-utility representation includes the second raw moment:
   $$
   V(\mu,\sigma^2)=\mu-b(\mu^2+\sigma^2)
   $$
   up to positive affine transformation.

4. **Recover the underlying NM utility.**

   Since
   $$
   E[X-bX^2]=E[X]-bE[X^2]
   =\mu-b(\sigma^2+\mu^2),
   $$
   the relevant NM utility must be quadratic, up to affine transformation:
   $$
   U(x)=x-bx^2.
   $$
   This is the paper's core exact result: if mean and variance summarize preferences over the whole mixture space, then quadratic utility is necessary.

5. **Explain why normality alone does not save the general theory.**

   Even if pure strategies generate normal returns, randomization across them generally does not preserve normality. Since the NM axioms are defined over mixtures, restricting the domain to normal pure strategies does not avoid the quadratic-utility conclusion unless one also restricts admissible mixtures.

6. **Separate weaker notions of consistency.**

   Baron then considers narrower questions:
   - Can mean-variance identify only the **optimal** choice, not the full ranking?
   - Can the result be widened when the admissible return distributions belong to special families?

   The answer is that the domain can be enlarged only by correspondingly restricting the choice set or the distribution family. The full unrestricted ranking result remains tied to quadratic utility.

7. **Economic implication.**

   Quadratic utility creates familiar pathologies:
   - increasing absolute risk aversion,
   - satiation beyond a finite wealth level,
   - problematic equilibrium implications.

   These are not side comments but the economic reason the foundation is restrictive rather than merely technical.

## 5. Domain of applicability

- The exact theorem applies when preferences satisfy the NM axioms on the full space generated by mixed strategies.
- If one only wants mean-variance as a local approximation or as a device on a restricted distribution family, the theorem is no longer decisive; Baron explicitly distinguishes these weaker regimes.
- The paper undercuts strong normative claims for global mean-variance rationality. It does **not** deny the usefulness of mean-variance as an approximation, engineering device, or special-case exact model.
- The proof supports necessity of quadratic utility for the full-ranking result. Broader claims that mean-variance is "generally inconsistent" need qualification by the relevant domain restrictions.


## 6. Three questions that must be kept separate

Baron organizes the article around three distinct demands on mean-variance analysis. The strongest demand is a complete ranking of pure alternatives and all randomized mixtures consistent with expected utility. The second is only to identify the preferred alternative, allowing mixtures that can never be optimal to be discarded. The third asks which mean-variance indifference curves can actually arise from expected utility when feasible payoffs belong to a restricted distribution family such as the normal family.

The distinction changes the answer. Quadratic utility is necessary for the unrestricted full-ranking claim treated by the paper. It is not necessary for many ordinary portfolio-selection problems in which payoffs are jointly normal and randomization among portfolios cannot improve on holding an appropriate portfolio directly. Finally, even on a normal family, one cannot choose any attractive-looking set of mean-variance indifference curves and assume it has an expected-utility foundation.

The paper is therefore more constructive than a blanket rejection of mean-variance analysis. Its restrictive theorem and its enlargement of the admissible utility class answer different questions. It expressly leaves the separate issue of approximation quality to the existing literature.

## 7. Randomizing across investments is not diversifying investments

Let $X_1,X_2$ be jointly defined security payoffs. A portfolio holds both and pays $X_a=qX_1+(1-q)X_2$. A randomized strategy first selects which single investment to hold and has distribution $F_Q=qF_1+(1-q)F_2$. These have the same mean but different variances:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_a)=q^2\sigma_1^2+(1-q)^2\sigma_2^2+2q(1-q)\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2,
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_Q)=q\sigma_1^2+(1-q)\sigma_2^2+q(1-q)(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2.
$$

Subtracting,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_Q)-\operatorname{Var}(X_a)
=q(1-q)\left[\operatorname{Var}(X_1-X_2)+(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2\right]
=q(1-q)E[(X_1-X_2)^2]\ge0.
$$

The difference vanishes only in a degenerate case or at endpoint allocations. This identity is the exact reason the lens of diversified payoffs in the paper's Figure 1 lies below the corresponding probability-mixture locus in mean-variance space.

Joint normality preserves normality under linear combinations, but not under random selection among distinct normal laws. A mixture of normals is generally nonnormal, although contrary to a loose statement in the source it need not be bimodal. Marginal normality of every security alone also does not establish joint normality. These qualifications preserve the argument without importing stronger distributional claims than are justified.

## 8. The quadratic representation and Borch's paradox

If $U(x)=x-bx^2$ with $b>0$, then

$$
EU(X)=\mu-b(\mu^2+\sigma^2).
$$

The missing $\mu^2$ term in the previous short summary was substantive: $\mu-b\sigma^2$ is a different criterion and does not follow from this quadratic utility. Indifference at level $k$ means

$$
\left(\mu-\frac1{2b}\right)^2+\sigma^2=\frac1{4b^2}-\frac{k}{b}.
$$

Thus the mean–standard-deviation diagram has concentric semicircles centered at $(1/(2b),0)$. Marginal utility is $U'(x)=1-2bx$, so monotonicity holds only on the payoff domain $x\le1/(2b)$. Absolute risk aversion is $2b/(1-2bx)$ and increases with wealth on that domain.

Borch's construction takes points said to be indifferent in the moment diagram and constructs two binary lotteries with those moments but a common payoff in one branch. Monotonicity then requires the other branch payoffs to be equal for indifference. Baron shows that the apparently paradoxical lotteries force at least one payoff into a region of negative quadratic marginal utility unless the moment pairs coincide. The conflict therefore reveals the limited payoff domain of quadratic utility. It does not invalidate a quadratic expected-utility model whose complete payoff support remains inside its increasing region.

Moment restrictions alone do not enforce that support restriction. A fixed mean and variance can arise from distributions with arbitrarily extreme tail locations. Therefore a pair of moments cannot by itself guarantee that every distribution sharing them is admissible under the economically monotone quadratic model. This is the substance of Proposition 2's caution about indifference across all distributions with the same moments.

## 9. Why the optimal-choice problem allows more utility functions

Proposition 3 uses the linearity of expected utility in the *distribution*. If the first pure alternative is preferred to the second,

$$
EU(X_Q)=qEU(X_1)+(1-q)EU(X_2)\le EU(X_1).
$$

Thus a best available pure alternative is at least as good as every randomized mixture of pure alternatives. Ranking all of those dominated mixtures is unnecessary to find the best action.

Proposition 4 gives a second route when the corresponding diversified payoff can be held. For concave utility, Jensen's inequality holds pointwise:

$$
U(qX_1+(1-q)X_2)\ge qU(X_1)+(1-q)U(X_2).
$$

Taking expectations proves that the portfolio weakly dominates the probability mixture in expected utility. With strict concavity, an interior mixture, and distinct payoffs on a set of positive probability, the inequality is strict. Convex utility reverses the ranking, and linear utility gives equality.

These two propositions do not rely on normality. Their role is to remove probability mixtures from the set of candidate optima. When the remaining relevant portfolio payoffs form a normal family, expected utility can be evaluated from their means and variances for a much wider class of utility functions. That is why the restrictive full-ranking proposition does not prohibit the standard normal-return portfolio model with nonquadratic risk aversion.

The availability conditions can fail. Baron gives currency-regime, bidding, and contingent-corporate-event examples. A firm facing a probability of a currency remaining in a narrow band or entering a floating regime cannot choose the preferred macro regime as a pure action and cannot directly hold a diversified average of the two conditional distributions. Similarly, a bidder chooses a bid that jointly changes winning probability and conditional profit. The resulting mixture is inherent in the action, so the dominated-randomization argument cannot simply discard it.

## 10. The heat-equation restriction for normal returns

Write $v=\sigma^2$ and $X=\mu+\sqrt vZ$ with $Z\sim N(0,1)$. For an integrable utility, define the actual cardinal expected-utility surface

$$
V(\mu,v)=E[U(\mu+\sqrt vZ)].
$$

Gaussian convolution satisfies

$$
2\frac{\partial V}{\partial v}=\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial\mu^2},
\qquad V(\mu,0)=U(\mu).
$$

The paper cites Allais and Chipman and states sufficient growth bounds to ensure existence and the converse representation. Bounds of Gaussian-exponential type must be accompanied by the variance and integrability restrictions needed for the convolution. The useful operational conclusion is that an arbitrary function of mean and variance need not be representable as expected utility.

For example, exponential utility $U(x)=-e^{-ax}$ gives

$$
V(\mu,v)=-e^{-a\mu+a^2v/2}.
$$

It satisfies the equation and has the same rankings as the certainty-equivalent criterion $\mu-av/2$. The certainty equivalent itself need not satisfy the heat equation because it is an ordinal transformation of the cardinal expected-utility surface. Checking the equation on an arbitrary chosen ordinal representation would therefore be the wrong test. The source discusses Chipman's stronger characterization allowing monotone transformations.

Baron uses the normal quantile criterion $H(\mu,\sigma)=\mu+\Phi^{-1}(p)\sigma$ to illustrate a limitation. For a lower quantile, its indifference lines are straight and parallel in mean–standard-deviation space. The article concludes that these lines do not correspond to a suitable expected-utility preference ordering on the normal family in the asserted generality. A safety-first objective can still be useful as a distinct decision criterion; it should not be called expected utility merely because its formula uses the same two moments.

## 11. Efficient sets and practical interpretation

For normal distributions, the familiar combination of higher mean and lower variance characterizes dominance for all increasing concave utility functions, with weak inequalities and at least one strict inequality in the nondegenerate comparison. This supplies a utility-theoretic interpretation of the normal mean-variance efficient set. The characterization does not automatically transfer to every two-parameter family; different tail shapes and support behavior can change rankings even when a distribution has two parameters.

The article notes that many alternative families also fail to remain in the same family under portfolio addition. That closure property is essential to a portfolio model: specifying each security's marginal distribution does not ensure a tractable distribution for all feasible linear combinations.

For a research or implementation decision, state whether the intended use is full preference ranking, optimal-action selection, efficient-set identification, or a local approximation. Then state the payoff family, attainable actions, mixture/portfolio distinction, utility support, and integrability requirements. The same quadratic program may be computationally useful under several interpretations, but the normative claim attached to it must match the actual domain. This article provides no empirical test of portfolio performance and no general error bound for mean-variance approximation; its contribution is to make those logical distinctions precise.
