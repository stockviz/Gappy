## 1. Metadata

- **Title:** Simplified Mean-Variance Portfolio Optimisation
- **Author(s):** Claudio Fontana and Martin Schweizer
- **Year:** 2012
- **Journal/Venue:** *Mathematics and Financial Economics*

## 2. Problem statement

The paper asks whether a broad class of discrete- or continuous-time mean-variance portfolio problems can be solved more cleanly by optimizing over **terminal positions** rather than over trading strategies directly. The target is a unified treatment of:

1. pure variance minimization,
2. mean-variance tradeoff with risk aversion,
3. target-mean problems,
4. target-variance problems,
5. and related indifference values,

under minimal no-arbitrage assumptions.

## 3. Approach (short)

The method is Hilbert-space projection. The set of attainable undiscounted terminal gains is closed to a linear subspace $G \subset L^2$, and the mean-variance problems are solved by projecting liabilities/endowments onto $G$ and the span of $1-\pi(1)$, where $\pi(1)$ is the orthogonal projection of the constant payoff $1$ onto $G^\perp$. The class of techniques is quadratic optimization in $L^2$, not dynamic programming or HJB.

## 4. Approach (detailed)

1. **Move from strategies to terminal gains.**

   Let $G$ denote the closed subspace of attainable discounted gains in $L^2$. Instead of choosing a trading strategy directly, choose $g\in G$, the final gain generated by the strategy. For a target/endowment $Y\in L^2$, one studies mean-variance problems for $g-Y$.

2. **Introduce the projection decomposition.**

   Let $g^Y$ be the orthogonal projection of $Y$ on $G$, and let
   $$
   N^Y = Y-g^Y
   $$
   be the orthogonal residual. Let $\pi(1)$ denote the projection of the constant payoff $1$ on $G^\perp$, the orthogonal complement of the closed gain space. Thus $1-\pi(1)$, rather than $\pi(1)$, is the attainable gain direction. Under the no-approximate-riskless-profit condition $1\notin G$, define
   $$
   a_Y^*=\frac{E[N^Y]}{E[\pi(1)]}.
   $$
   Then the minimum-variance hedge for $Y$ is
   $$
   g_{mv}^Y = g^Y - a_Y^*(1-\pi(1)).
   $$

3. **Solve the base variance-minimization problem.**

   The core problem
   $$
   \min_{g\in G}\operatorname{Var}(Y-g)
   $$
   has the unique solution $g_{mv}^Y$. This is the analogue of the global minimum-variance hedge.

4. **Solve the mean-variance utility problem.**

   For risk tolerance $\gamma=1/\alpha$, solve
   $$
   \min_{g\in G} \left\{\operatorname{Var}(g-Y)-\gamma E[g-Y]\right\}.
   $$
   The solution is
   $$
   g_{opt,A}^Y(\gamma)
   = g_{mv}^Y + \frac{\gamma}{2E[\pi(1)]}(1-\pi(1)).
   $$
   Thus every efficient solution is the minimum-variance hedge plus a multiple of a single direction $1-\pi(1)$.

5. **Solve the target-mean and target-variance problems.**

   For a lower-bound target mean $\mu>E[R_{mv}^Y]$, the optimizer is
   $$
   g_{opt,B}^Y(\mu)
   = g_{mv}^Y + \frac{\mu - E[R_{mv}^Y]}{E[1-\pi(1)]}(1-\pi(1)),
   $$
   and for target variance $\sigma^2$, the optimizer is
   $$
   g_{opt,C}^Y(\sigma^2)
   = g_{mv}^Y
   + \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2-\operatorname{Var}(R_{mv}^Y)}
                 {\operatorname{Var}[1-\pi(1)]}}
     (1-\pi(1)),
   $$
   in the admissible parameter range.

6. **Interpret the geometry.**

   All efficient solutions live on an affine line in $L^2$: once the minimum-variance point is known, every other mean-variance efficient payoff is obtained by moving along the single direction $1-\pi(1)$. This yields two-fund separation in the relevant payoff space.

7. **Extend to indifference values.**

   Because the optimization problems have explicit solutions, quadratic, mean, and variance indifference values for contingent claims can be written explicitly by comparing the optimal objective with and without the claim.

### Proof sketch

The variance can be written as $\min_{a\in\mathbb R} E[(X-a)^2]$, which reduces mean-variance problems to orthogonal projection problems in $L^2$. The projection theorem then gives the unique minimizers. The key object $1-\pi(1)$ appears because $1$ need not belong to the attainable-gain space $G$; its projection gap is the unique direction that trades off mean against variance.

## 5. Domain of applicability

The method applies in discrete or continuous time to a linear gain space in $L^2$ under a mild no-approximate-riskless-profit condition. Exact optimizers are obtained in its closure; attainability in the original trading space requires a separate check. Its strength is model-independence: many specific diffusion or semimartingale models are just special cases. Its limitations are also clear: it is quadratic-only, terminal-wealth focused, and depends on $L^2$ structure. Nonquadratic preferences or genuinely path-dependent objectives are outside its scope.

It does not require a diffusion, a Brownian filtration, or a specified trading strategy class beyond what is needed to describe a linear set of square-integrable gains.

To remove a potentially serious ambiguity, write $\mathcal G$ for the original attainable-gain space and $G=\overline{\mathcal G}^{L^2}$ for its closure. The paper's operator $\pi$ projects onto $G^\perp$, **not** onto $G$. Consequently $g^Y=Y-\pi(Y)$ lies in $G$, while $N^Y=\pi(Y)$ is orthogonal to gains. Reversing that convention makes the displayed portfolio formulas infeasible. This corrects the notation in the earlier short summary.

An optimum in the closure need only be approximable by actual strategies. The distinction is central to what the paper delivers: positions and values can be characterized without constructing the strategy that attains them. Closedness or an explicit replication theorem is additional financial-model work.

## 7. Assumptions and the role of the numeraire

The gain space is linear, representing frictionless unrestricted trading from zero initial capital. The core condition is $1\notin G$, meaning no sequence of zero-cost gains can converge in $L^2$ to a deterministic positive payoff. It rules out approximate riskless profits but is weaker than many standard no-arbitrage conditions: it does not itself assert that every nonnegative nonzero payoff is unattainable.

A second assumption, $1\notin G^\perp$, ensures that some gains have nonzero expectation under the physical measure. Without it, all gains have zero mean and the mean-variance tradeoff degenerates. The variance-minimization and penalized problems still have natural solutions, but the formulas dividing by the expected efficient gain require the nondegenerate case.

The terminal payoff $B>0$ of an asset costing one initially can be random. The authors avoid calling it riskless. Attainable wealths with arbitrary initial capital form $\mathcal A=\mathbb RB+\mathcal G$. For the core optimization, $B$ enters through the liability/endowment $Y$; the projection geometry itself works directly on undiscounted random variables.

Define

$$
z=\pi(1),\qquad k=1-z,\qquad p=E[z],\qquad q=1-p.
$$

Orthogonality gives

$$
p=E[z^2]=\|z\|_2^2,
\qquad q=E[k^2]=\|k\|_2^2,
\qquad \operatorname{Var}(k)=pq.
$$

Under the two nondegeneracy assumptions, $0<p<1$ and $q>0$. These identities explain the signs and denominators in all the formulas. In particular, $z$ need not be pointwise nonnegative even though its expectation is positive.

## 8. Minimum variance is not the same as least-squares hedging

The quadratic hedge minimizes $E[(Y-g)^2]$ and is simply $g^Y=Y-N^Y$. Variance minimization permits a nonzero mean hedge error and therefore solves a different problem. Introduce a scalar $a$:

$$
\min_{g\in G}\operatorname{Var}(Y-g)
=\min_a\min_{g\in G}E[(Y-a-g)^2]
=\min_a E[(N^Y-az)^2].
$$

Because $E[N^Yz]=E[N^Y]$, completing the square yields

$$
a_Y^*=\frac{E[N^Y]}p,
\qquad g_{mv}^Y=g^Y-a_Y^*k.
$$

The optimized net result $R_{mv}^Y=g_{mv}^Y-Y$ has

$$
m_0:=E[R_{mv}^Y]=-\frac{E[N^Y]}p,
\qquad
v_0:=\operatorname{Var}(R_{mv}^Y)
=E[(N^Y)^2]-\frac{E[N^Y]^2}{p}.
$$

Its defining property is $\operatorname{Cov}(R_{mv}^Y,g)=0$ for every $g\in G$. This is covariance orthogonality, not necessarily $L^2$ orthogonality. The least-squares residual $N^Y$ has the latter property; the minimum-variance result has the former.

The distinction is financially useful. A hedge that minimizes squared error penalizes systematic over- or under-hedging in mean. A variance-minimizing hedge removes fluctuations while allowing a constant expected shortfall or surplus. Initial capital and claim pricing determine whether that residual mean is acceptable.

## 9. Reducing every optimization to one scalar

Let $\mathcal N=\{n\in G:E[n]=0\}$. The expected-value functional on $G$ is represented by $k$ because $E[g]=E[gk]$. Hence every attainable closed-space gain can be written uniquely as

$$
g=g_{mv}^Y+ck+n,\qquad n\in\mathcal N.
$$

The net result then satisfies

$$
E[g-Y]=m_0+cq,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(g-Y)=v_0+c^2pq+\operatorname{Var}(n).
$$

The zero-mean direction $n$ adds variance without adding mean, so it vanishes at an efficient solution. The full infinite-dimensional problem reduces to choosing $c$.

For risk tolerance $\gamma=1/\alpha$, minimizing variance minus $\gamma$ times mean gives $c=\gamma/(2p)$. For a lower-bound mean target $\mu$, the solution is

$$
c_B=\frac{(\mu-m_0)^+}{q}.
$$

The positive part is essential: if $\mu\le m_0$, the minimum-variance result already meets the constraint and no negative tilt is required. An equality-constrained target below $m_0$ would be a different problem and would select the lower branch.

For maximum variance $s^2$, feasibility requires $s^2\ge v_0$ and

$$
c_C=\sqrt{\frac{s^2-v_0}{pq}}.
$$

At equality $s^2=v_0$, the unique solution is the minimum-variance point. Below it, no feasible gain can satisfy the variance cap. These cases are part of the theorem, not optional implementation details.

The quadratic hedge is also on the same affine line, since $g^Y=g_{mv}^Y+a_Y^*k$. However $a_Y^*$ can be negative. Thus a least-squares hedge can lie on the lower branch of the mean-variance locus; the positive-square-root upper-frontier relation should not be indiscriminately applied to it.

## 10. Frontier, separation, and beta relations

Eliminating $c$ gives the frontier equation

$$
v=v_0+\frac p q(m-m_0)^2.
$$

On the upper efficient branch,

$$
m=m_0+\sqrt{v-v_0}\sqrt{q/p}.
$$

This is two-fund separation in terminal-payoff space: the liability-specific hedge $g_{mv}^Y$ and the universal gain $k$ span all optimal positions for the penalized and constrained problems. The funds may be dynamic trading outcomes rather than static vectors of stock weights.

Every gain also obeys the identity

$$
E[g]=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(g,k)}{\operatorname{Var}(k)}E[k].
$$

The paper develops related zero-beta representations for net positions $g-Y$ and an abstract CAPM construction when investors share the same endowment and differ in mean targets. Averaging their efficient choices yields another efficient choice. This is an equilibrium-style implication under those common-opportunity and common-endowment assumptions, not an empirical claim that an observed market index necessarily equals $k$.

For a maximum Sharpe-ratio net result, the source requires $m_0>0$ and $v_0>0$. Then

$$
g_{sr}^Y=g_{mv}^Y+\frac{v_0}{m_0p}k.
$$

If $m_0<0$, that stationary expression instead minimizes the ratio. Boundary and zero-mean cases must be treated separately; the formula is not a universal tangency portfolio for every liability.

## 11. Indifference valuation and unhedgeable risk

To interpret selling a claim $H$ for cash $h$ with initial capital $c$ and an existing terminal position $H_0$, set

$$
Y=-(c+h)B+H-H_0.
$$

For simple explicit valuation formulas, the paper imposes a further assumption: a unit terminal payoff is approximately attainable at positive initial cost $\delta$, so $1=\delta B+\bar g$ for $\bar g\in G$ and $\delta>0$. This assumption is not needed for the earlier projection solution. It implies $\pi(1)=\delta\pi(B)$ and permits the decomposition

$$
H=c_HB+\widetilde g_H+L_H,
\qquad
c_H=\frac{E[H\pi(B)]}{E[B\pi(B)]},
$$

where $\widetilde g_H\in G$ and $L_H$ is orthogonal to all attainable wealths. In particular $E[L_H]=0$. The first part is the attainable approximation to the claim, while $L_H$ is its unhedgeable residual.

For mean-variance utility $E[X]-\alpha\operatorname{Var}(X)$, the seller's indifference value is

$$
h_A(H)=c_H+\delta\alpha
\left[\operatorname{Var}(L_H)-2\operatorname{Cov}(L_H,L_{H_0})\right].
$$

The first term is the replication/approximation value of the hedgeable part. The second compensates for the incremental residual risk of selling the claim against the existing book. Initial cash $c$ drops out of this particular quadratic-preference value; the unhedgeable composition of the existing book does not.

A positive residual covariance with the existing book can reduce the seller's risk charge because selling $H$ offsets that book. For fixed $\alpha$, the difference between seller and buyer values is $2\delta\alpha\operatorname{Var}(L_H)$; the existing-book covariance cancels from the spread. For an approximately attainable claim, $L_H=0$ and the preference-dependent adjustment disappears.

For the target-mean and target-variance criteria, analogous values have the same replication-plus-incremental-risk structure but an effective risk-aversion coefficient that can depend on the claim. They can be infinite when unhedgeable residual risk already exceeds the relevant risk budget. Extra cash cannot eliminate a residual variance that is orthogonal to every attainable wealth direction.

The values also satisfy a sequential-sale consistency property when both initial cash and the existing book are updated after the first sale. They are subjective reservation values, not automatically liquid-market prices.

## 12. Why discounting is not innocuous for this objective

If $B$ is random, minimizing undiscounted variance and minimizing variance of $X/B$ are different preferences. A change of measure with density $B^2/E[B^2]$ can preserve squared-error norms up to scale:

$$
E^{P^B}[(X/B)^2]=E[X^2]/E[B^2].
$$

But its mean is $E^{P^B}[X/B]=E[BX]/E[B^2]$, which is not the original physical expectation $E[X]$. Therefore a transformation convenient for quadratic hedging does not automatically preserve a mean-variance investment problem. The authors explicitly highlight this issue as a reason to work undiscounted.

For $B\equiv1$, the variance-optimal signed martingale density is

$$
\widetilde D=\frac{\pi(1)}{E[\pi(1)]}.
$$

It has expectation one and annihilates the gain space in the appropriate $L^2$ sense. The word “signed” matters: the weak assumptions do not ensure pointwise positivity, so it need not define an equivalent probability measure.

## 13. Implementation and limitations

In a finite scenario model, represent gains by columns of a payoff matrix and use probability-weighted least squares to compute the projections of $1$ and $Y$. Check the residual normal equations, $0<p<1$, the minimum-variance covariance orthogonality, and the target feasibility conditions. Those calculations recover the entire frontier without separately optimizing each target.

Recovering actual trades remains a distinct problem. In continuous time this generally requires a model-specific martingale representation, stochastic control, or hedging construction; an abstract optimal terminal payoff does not supply rebalancing instructions. If only a closure optimum exists, a sequence of approximating strategies may have undesirable leverage or turnover despite converging in terminal $L^2$.

Linearity excludes position bounds, many solvency restrictions, transaction costs, and other nonlinear trading constraints. Square integrability excludes sufficiently heavy-tailed payoffs. Terminal variance ignores pathwise drawdowns and liquidity needs. Mean-variance preferences are not monotone in every statewise improvement, so reservation values can behave perversely for some nonnegative claims; the source discusses negative values and possible constraints that sacrifice its simple formulas.

There is no empirical backtest. The contribution is a unification of several exact quadratic problems, with explicit assumptions and a clean separation between the model-independent geometry of terminal payoffs and the model-dependent task of trading to attain them.
