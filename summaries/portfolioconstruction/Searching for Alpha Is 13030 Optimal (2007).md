## 1. Metadata

- **Title:** Searching for Alpha: Is 130/30 Optimal?
- **Author(s):** Manolis Liodakis
- **Year:** 2007
- **Journal/Venue:** Citigroup Quantitative Analysis report

## 2. Problem statement

The paper asks an implementation question for active equity management: **if a manager is allowed to add a balanced long-short “short extension” to a long-only mandate, what level of leverage is optimal, and why is the industry standard $130/30$ not generally the optimizer?** The relevant objective is not raw alpha but alpha expression subject to tracking-error constraints.

## 3. Approach (short)

The method is constrained active-portfolio geometry plus simulation. Starting from a factor-risk-model optimizer with a tracking-error target, the paper studies how a leverage parameter $\ell$ changes the portfolio’s ability to express buy and sell views. It introduces “useful active weight” as signed active capital aligned with buy and sell recommendations rather than neutral positions, and then characterizes the leverage $\ell^\star$ that maximizes useful active weight.

## 4. Approach (detailed)

1. **Long-only active portfolio as baseline**

   Let $w$ denote active weights relative to a benchmark, with alpha vector $\alpha$ and risk model $\Sigma$. The baseline optimization is of the usual form
   $$
   \max_w \alpha^\top w
   \qquad \text{s.t.} \qquad
   w^\top \Sigma w \le \tau^2,
   $$
   together with long-only and benchmark-related constraints. When $\tau$ is small, many negative views cannot be expressed directly and must be encoded as underweights.

2. **Short extension**

   Introduce leverage $\ell\in[0,\bar \ell]$. A $100+\ell / \ell$ portfolio holds $100+\ell$% long and $\ell$% short. The active portfolio can therefore carry explicit short positions in sell ideas, with the long side re-expanded to keep net investment at 100%; market beta requires separate control.

3. **Useful active weight**

   The key concept in the report is that not all active weight is equally valuable. Some active risk is spent on “deadweight” holdings used only to offset the risk generated by genuine alpha positions. The paper therefore distinguishes:

   - active weight on buy ideas;
   - active weight on sell ideas;
   - active weight on neutralizing names.

   “Useful active weight” is the part allocated to the first two categories.

4. **Why leverage helps initially**

   At low $\ell$, extra shorting improves the expression of negative views and often improves diversification of active bets. For a given tracking-error budget $\tau$, this lets the optimizer allocate a larger share of risk to alpha-bearing names.

5. **Why leverage eventually hurts**

   Because the portfolio still faces a tracking-error cap, higher leverage eventually forces the optimizer to offset the new long-short activity by:

   - scaling down strong buy positions;
   - introducing neutral or low-alpha names for risk control;
   - using more “deadweight” positions.

   Therefore useful active weight is not monotone in $\ell$. The fixed-extension examples can exhibit an interior optimum
   $$
   \ell^\star = \arg\max_{\ell} UAW(\ell),
   $$
   where $UAW$ denotes useful active weight.

6. **Determinants of $\ell^\star$**

   The report shows that $\ell^\star$ depends materially on:

   - the tracking-error target $\tau$;
   - breadth of alpha opportunities;
   - symmetry between buy and sell lists;
   - benchmark concentration;
   - strength and diversification of the alpha model.

   In general, tighter tracking-error budgets imply smaller optimal leverage because the incremental long-short activity must be neutralized more aggressively.

7. **Efficient-frontier interpretation**

   For each fixed $\ell$, one can trace an active efficient frontier in $(\text{tracking error}, \text{alpha exposure})$ space. The report’s figures show that higher leverage shifts the frontier outward only over a restricted range of tracking-error budgets. Thus 130/30 can dominate 100/0 in some regions but be dominated by lower-leverage designs in others.

8. **Proof status**

   There is no general closed-form theorem for $\ell^\star$. The argument combines:

   - exact quadratic active-risk geometry;
   - the definition of useful active weight;
   - numerical optimization and simulation over many alpha sets.

   The paper’s strong claim is therefore empirical/constructive: 130/30 is not universally optimal once one accounts for tracking-error constraints and the need to spend risk budget on neutralizing positions.

## 5. Domain of applicability

- The analysis applies to **benchmark-relative active equity management** with short extension.
- It is most relevant for managers subject to tracking-error limits and long-biased mandates.
- The exact value of $\ell^\star$ is model- and mandate-specific; the report illustrates nonmonotonicity under prescribed extension levels; it proves no universal leverage constant.
- The report explicitly analyzes trading and borrowing costs through stylized scenarios; it does not model stock-specific impact and funding frictions comprehensively.


## 6. The quantity being optimized and a necessary correction

This is a 36-page Citigroup research report dated 26 June 2007, led by Manolis Liodakis and carrying the wider quantitative research team's names. Its central diagnostic, **useful active weight**, is a sum of portfolio weights assigned in the direction of buy and sell recommendations. It is not a contribution to portfolio variance or a risk-budget share.

For absolute holdings $p$, benchmark weights $b$, and active weights $x=p-b$, a simplified symmetric discrete-score model has $\alpha_i=a$ on the buy list, $\alpha_i=-a$ on the sell list, and zero on neutral names. Then

$$\alpha'x=a\left(\sum_{i\in B}x_i-\sum_{i\in S}x_i\right).$$

When positions follow the recommendations, the bracket is the report's useful active capital. If positions oppose a recommendation, their signed contribution must be retained. With a common payoff magnitude on both sides, maximizing this exposure also maximizes modeled gross alpha. If alpha magnitudes differ across names or between the buy and sell lists, unweighted useful active capital is no longer sufficient; the actual alpha-weighted objective is needed.

For net investment one, short exposure $s$ implies long exposure $1+s$ and gross exposure $1+2s$. Thus 130/30 means 160% gross and 100% net. It does not by itself ensure benchmark beta one. The report uses “leverage” to mean the extension or short amount, which differs from gross exposure and from a scalar multiple of the entire portfolio.

### 6.1 An interior optimum depends on the mandate formulation

The report compares prescribed extension levels under tracking-error restrictions. Forcing additional short exposure can push the optimizer into neutral holdings and reduce useful alpha exposure. But allowing a larger maximum short amount cannot reduce the maximum value of an unchanged objective: the smaller-cap portfolio remains feasible. Formally, if $\mathcal F(s_1)\subseteq\mathcal F(s_2)$, then

$$\max_{p\in\mathcal F(s_2)}\alpha'(p-b)
\ge\max_{p\in\mathcal F(s_1)}\alpha'(p-b).$$

Therefore the report's hump-shaped fixed-extension curves do not prove that greater optional flexibility is harmful. They show that an imposed structure can overshoot the useful amount of shorting. Even with optional shorting, the selected exposure may be interior because additional positions add no gross value or because costs outweigh their benefit. That is a different statement from a declining optimized gross objective as an upper limit is relaxed.

## 7. Reading the worked example

The initial illustrative manager has 100 buy ideas and 100 sell ideas in MSCI Europe. They represent 19.8% and 22.9% of benchmark capitalization; neutral stocks represent 57.3%. At 3% tracking error the long-only portfolio holds only buy-list stocks, creating 80.2% aggregate buy overweights and underweights of 22.9% in sell names and 57.3% in neutral names.

Useful active weight is therefore $80.2+22.9=103.1\%$, against total absolute active weight of 160.4%, giving a useful fraction of about 64%. Neither number is a variance fraction. Neutral underweights are called “waste” because the assumed alpha model assigns them no expected payoff, although they may still serve an important risk-control purpose.

The 130/30 version has 88% buy overweights and 52.9% sell underweights. Useful active weight rises to 140.9%, about 77% of the reported 184% total absolute active weight. The gain comes mainly from expressing negative views more strongly, especially in stocks whose benchmark weights would otherwise cap their underweights.

As extension increases, buy exposure peaks around 19%, while combined useful buy-and-sell exposure peaks near 35%. Beyond the latter point, additional sell exposure is more than offset by the reduction of buy exposure necessary to respect the fixed risk target. These are illustrative thresholds for the particular lists and risk model, not estimated population constants.

## 8. The determinants of a suitable extension

The report's simulations use 2006 data, sets of randomly selected buy and sell lists, a specified hit rate, and a factor risk model. This design explores variation across opportunity sets conditional on a calibration. It is not a test of a prospectively selected strategy over many independent market regimes.

**Tracking error.** Higher risk budgets generally accommodate more extension before hedging or neutral positions become necessary. At low tracking error, forcing a large short sleeve can make the holdings primarily a risk-control construction rather than a direct expression of forecasts.

**Benchmark weight of sell ideas.** Negative views on large benchmark names can already be expressed with meaningful underweights. Negative views on tiny constituents or off-benchmark stocks gain more from short permission. The usefulness of extension depends on the location of the signal, not merely on overall index concentration.

**Number and covariance of ideas.** More genuinely distinct buy and sell opportunities can improve diversification within the forecast-bearing lists and reduce the need for neutral stocks. Counting names alone is insufficient when their risks are concentrated in common factors. The report also compares discrete buy/sell lists with continuous scores; changing the score representation changes both the opportunity set and its implied payoff assumptions.

**Asymmetric skill.** In the worked example, symmetric skill supports about 35% extension; no skill on the sell side shifts the useful optimum to about 19%; stronger buy skill gives about 26%; stronger sell skill gives about 44%. These values result from the source's assumed relative payoffs. A common scaling of all alphas leaves a cost-free exposure optimum unchanged under a fixed risk target, but absolute skill matters once costs are introduced.

**Return magnitudes and market conditions.** A hit rate is not an expected-return forecast. The report's 2006 discussion notes that winning and losing stocks had unequal average magnitudes and that small-cap performance affected short extensions. Correctly ranking slightly more than half the names is insufficient if errors have systematically larger payoffs than successes. A signal calibrated only to classification accuracy may therefore misstate the benefits of shorting.

## 9. The World Radar Screen case and costs

The World Radar Screen combines value and momentum and ranks MSCI Europe stocks. Using the top and bottom quintiles at the start of 2006 gives roughly 130 names on each side. At 2% tracking error, useful exposure peaks around 34% extension, whereas buy exposure alone peaks near 20%. For the same quintile implementation, reported optima are approximately 23.3% at 1.5% tracking error, 45% at 2.5%, and the tested 50% ceiling at 3%. A boundary solution at 50% does not establish that the unconstrained optimum is exactly 50%.

The authors also test narrower decile lists and continuous percentile scores. Different representations yield different extension levels, reinforcing that “130/30” is a packaging choice rather than an implication of mean–variance theory.

To incorporate costs, the report assumes about 9% annual benchmark-relative payoff for each favorable side of the quintile strategy. Multiplying this by 122% useful active exposure at roughly 34% extension gives about 11% modeled gross excess return. This calculation is an assumption-based translation of historical model performance, not a guaranteed prospective alpha.

The low, medium, and high cost scenarios respectively use trading costs of 5, 10, and 20 basis points, and annual borrowing costs of 30, 50, and 100 basis points. Turnover is high: the illustration assumes roughly 80% monthly for long-only and 160% for 150/50 under its convention. The resulting preferred extensions fall from about 34% before costs to 32%, 31%, and 26%. These scenarios are generic; the report explicitly notes that a stock-specific impact-cost model inside the optimization would be more rigorous.

The original note's description of the report as weak on all financing frictions would miss this explicit cost analysis. Its limitation is that the analysis uses stylized average costs and turnover assumptions rather than a comprehensive implementation model with stock-level borrow availability, nonlinear impact, fund size, recalls, and dynamic rebalancing.

## 10. Interpretation and scope

The report's UCITS III discussion reflects the 2007 setting and the authors' description of the rules then applicable. It should be read as historical context, not as a statement of current regulation. The economic argument survives independently of those historical numerical limits.

For a practical study, jointly optimize holdings and optional extension under a risk ceiling, then compare the result with any externally imposed long–short structure. Use separate estimates of buy and sell alpha, include costs at the security level where possible, and test sensitivity to covariance and alpha errors. Report both absolute active capital and forecast active risk so that an apparent efficiency improvement is not attributed to the wrong quantity.

The source's strongest conclusion is that the desirable short extension depends on signal location, risk tolerance, implementation costs, and buy/sell asymmetry. It provides numerical demonstrations and a design framework; it does not prove a universal interior optimum or establish that the standard 130/30 structure is generally inefficient.
